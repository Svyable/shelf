# WE STOLE THE NIGHT

*How Artificial Light Remade the Living World*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

On an October morning in Chicago, nearly a thousand migrating birds lay dead beneath the glass of a convention center. A new film on that glass later helped reduce collisions by more than 95 percent. The building remained. The city kept operating. Someone changed the design.

That is the central story of this book: not an argument that civilization should turn its lights off, but a question about what happens when a technology becomes so easy to use that we stop asking where its consequences go.

Artificial illumination changed how we work, move, trade, and care for one another. It expanded the useful hours of cities, improved many public spaces, and helped make modern medicine and infrastructure possible. Its benefits are real. But light does not stop at the pavement or doorway it was purchased to illuminate. It crosses bedrooms, migratory routes, flowering meadows, lakes, reefs, and the sky itself. Some of those effects are measured. Others remain uncertain. None can be settled by calling brightness progress or darkness virtue.

*We Stole the Night* moves from the early gaslight streets of London to satellite maps of a brightening planet, from biological clocks to night-shift labor, from insects visiting flowers after dusk to turtles following the wrong horizon. It places experimental evidence for lighting's public-safety benefits beside studies of its environmental costs. It makes room for a crossing that genuinely needs more light and a façade that has no reason to shine until dawn.

The question is not how little light a society can endure. It is how precisely a mature society can use it.

The research record linked in this edition distinguishes peer-reviewed primary experiments, observational studies, scientific reviews, institutional guidance, historical sources, and explicitly hypothetical examples. The case studies are cited directly in the prose and indexed in the source ledger. Several 2026 findings were verified against their primary publications before this first edition was prepared. No global biological effect is inferred from a single local experiment.
