# 16 — Making a City Less Bright

The first useful question in a lighting audit is not how many lamps a city owns.

It is what those lamps are supposed to accomplish.

An exterior fixture may illuminate a road, a staircase, a doorway, a public square, a sign, a wall, a playing field, or a parking area. Each task requires different visual conditions. The brightness needed for a pedestrian crossing is not necessarily the brightness needed for a decorative façade. A hospital entrance may need reliable overnight lighting; an empty office floor probably does not need every perimeter lamp on until dawn.

Yet cities often inherit lighting as a collection of equipment rather than as a clear set of services. Fixtures are installed through projects, replaced when they fail, and controlled according to old schedules. A system can become brighter through a series of ordinary maintenance decisions without any public decision to brighten the city as a whole.

There is a better starting point.

DarkSky International and the Illuminating Engineering Society have articulated [five principles for responsible outdoor lighting](https://darksky.org/RESOURCES/guides-and-how-tos/lighting-principles/): useful, targeted, low-level, controlled, and warm-colored where possible. The principles are intentionally practical. They do not demand the abolition of outdoor light. They ask whether its purpose, direction, amount, timing, and spectrum are appropriate.

Those five questions provide a useful framework for a city-scale lighting plan. But a framework is not an implementation. It has to be translated into inventories, performance requirements, maintenance practices, and evaluations that protect legitimate safety needs.

A city can become less bright without becoming less usable. That is the architectural opportunity of modern lighting.

## Begin with a map of obligations

A municipal lighting inventory should contain more than fixture locations. It should identify the owner, intended task, lamp type, optical distribution, output, spectrum where available, operating schedule, controls, and maintenance status.

Some information will be incomplete. Public streetlights may be well documented while private floodlights, signs, and commercial facilities contribute substantial output outside municipal control. A city may know what it has purchased but not how much light it receives from every neighboring property.

The inventory is still valuable because it reveals obvious opportunities. A bright fixture may illuminate an empty lot. Two nearby sources may overlap unnecessarily. A lamp may point across a property boundary. An older unit may have no effective shielding. A lighting system may remain at full output through hours when activity is low.

The audit should also identify needs that are not being met. A dark transit stop or dangerous crossing may require better illumination. A resident's concern about safety cannot be dismissed because the project has an environmental goal. A city can reduce waste in one place and improve functional light in another.

This makes the process different from a simple lamp-reduction target. The goal is not to count how many fixtures were removed. It is to provide the necessary nighttime functions with less unwanted exposure and avoidable expense.

The inventory should be connected to measurements. Lumen ratings and watts are useful but insufficient. Illuminance, luminance, glare, and spectral characteristics may matter for specific tasks. Ground-based sky measurements and local ecological observations can provide additional evidence where appropriate.

A city with only one number—total electricity consumption—can make progress on energy while missing other parts of the problem.

## The beam that knows where to go

Targeting is often the most straightforward improvement. A lamp intended to light a path should concentrate output on the path rather than sending substantial light into the sky, neighboring windows, or nearby habitat.

Shielding and careful optics can improve that distribution. Modern LED fixtures offer substantial control over beam shape, though the quality of the result depends on the product and installation. A badly aimed LED can cause glare and spill just as surely as an older lamp.

The technical design begins with the task. A pedestrian needs to recognize the path, hazards, and nearby people. A driver needs adequate contrast and visibility across a route. A loading dock requires sufficient light for workers and equipment. The engineer must choose the distribution that supports those uses without assuming all surrounding surfaces need equal illumination.

This can produce a counterintuitive result: less generated light may provide better useful visibility. A bright bare source may waste output above the horizon and create discomfort glare. A controlled source may deliver adequate illumination where needed with lower total flux.

Such improvements can also reduce energy costs. They may avoid the need to overcompensate for poor distribution by specifying a stronger lamp.

There are limits. Some applications require broad lighting or high levels, and a narrow beam can create undesirable shadows if not designed properly. A city should not treat full shielding as a substitute for evaluating uniformity and sightlines.

The goal is a beam that serves its assigned task, not a lamp that never allows a photon to escape.

## The hour that changes the need

Control is the other major opportunity. A fixture can be useful at one time and unnecessary at another. An office entrance may need light while staff are arriving, then a lower level after closing. A sports field may need intense illumination during an event and little afterward. A residential roadway may have variable traffic through the night.

Timers and dimming can respond to these patterns. Motion or occupancy sensors can also be useful in some settings, though they require careful evaluation. Sudden illumination can disturb animals or surprise users. A sensor may fail to detect a person. A control system that becomes unreliable can undermine safety.

The simplest robust schedule may sometimes be better than a complicated adaptive system. A predictable part-night dimming plan can be easier to maintain and explain. In other locations, responsive controls can provide higher output exactly when the task demands it.

The key is to measure the actual function before choosing the schedule. A road used by overnight workers cannot be treated as empty because most residents are asleep. A warehouse may need continuous task lighting even when the offices are closed. A park with sensitive insect activity may benefit from changes during a particular evening window.

There is no universal moment after which all light becomes waste. The duration of need is site-specific.

The 2026 insect attraction study provides an ecological reason to think beyond aggregate operating hours. If insects respond differently during different periods of the night, two schedules with the same total hours can create different exposures. The timing of a lamp is part of its environmental footprint.

A responsible system knows not only where light is needed but when.

## Color without mythology

Spectrum matters, but public conversation has turned it into a collection of simplified rules. Warm light is described as safe; blue light as dangerous. Neither claim holds without considering intensity, timing, geometry, and the organisms involved.

Lower correlated color temperatures often reduce the share of short-wavelength emission relative to cooler white sources, but CCT is not a complete spectral measure. Two products with the same CCT can differ. Different species have different sensitivities. A warm floodlight aimed at a turtle nesting beach may still be harmful.

A good specification uses the lowest short-wavelength output compatible with the actual visual task where sensitive exposure is a concern. It also controls direction and duration. For some tasks, color rendering and recognition are important; the appropriate spectrum must serve those needs.

The policy challenge is to avoid turning one product label into a claim of ecological absolution. Manufacturers may describe fixtures as dark-sky-friendly based on particular optical or spectral characteristics. The installation still needs to be evaluated in context.

A city should also document what it actually buys and installs. Replacement products may change over time. A poorly controlled procurement process can produce inconsistent spectra and outputs across neighborhoods.

The goal is not a universal color. It is a defensible relationship between illumination and purpose.

## The maintenance test

A lighting design exists on paper for a brief moment. The installed system may remain in use for decades.

Controls can be overridden. Shields can be damaged. Trees can grow into or away from light paths. A fixture can be replaced with a different optical distribution. A property owner can add a new floodlight. A timer can fail. A lighting ordinance can be formally passed and then rarely enforced.

This means that responsible light is partly a maintenance problem. A city must be able to inspect installations, detect failures, and maintain a clear standard for replacements. It may need to train contractors and utility crews in the environmental goals of the design.

An overcomplicated system can perform poorly if staff lack the resources to manage it. A simple, well-shielded fixture with a reliable schedule may deliver more durable benefits than a sophisticated adaptive network that is frequently bypassed.

The same principle applies to safety. An automated dimming system must fail into an appropriate state under plausible conditions. A pedestrian crossing should not become hazardous because a sensor misses one person. Emergency overrides should be reliable, and their operation should not become a permanent excuse to leave everything at maximum output.

Performance should be monitored after installation. Electricity savings alone cannot verify reduced glare or ecological exposure. Measurements at relevant locations can show whether the project achieved its intended outcomes.

The work becomes more effective when environmental and safety objectives are part of ordinary maintenance rather than a one-time conservation campaign.

## The purchasing specification that changes a city

Municipal procurement can influence large amounts of light. A city purchasing thousands of fixtures can require optical control, appropriate spectra, dimming compatibility, maintenance documentation, and evidence of meeting relevant visibility standards.

These specifications may produce more change than a campaign asking individual residents to turn off porch lights. Public infrastructure sets expectations and can demonstrate what responsible illumination looks like.

The specification should be performance-based where possible. Instead of rewarding maximum lumens or minimum price alone, it can define the necessary task illumination, limits on unwanted output, expected operating schedules, and maintenance requirements. It can invite bidders to meet the objectives efficiently.

A good process also accounts for total cost. A fixture that is cheap to purchase but expensive to maintain may be a poor choice. A sophisticated control system that requires frequent repairs may erase operational savings. The environmental benefit should be assessed against the actual service life and use.

The [DOE's federal exterior-lighting guidance](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-exterior-lighting) provides a model for treating luminaire efficacy and controls as procurement considerations. Environmental and site-specific requirements can be added to the conventional efficiency framework.

The city does not need to invent a consciousness of darkness. It needs a clearer set of contractual obligations for the light it buys.

## The small demonstration with a large lesson

Imagine a municipal pilot on a mixed residential and commercial corridor. Before changes, the city records fixture characteristics, nighttime traffic, complaints about glare, pedestrian visibility, and sky brightness at several locations. It identifies a crossing where illumination is inadequate and nearby lamps that waste light into windows and the sky.

The redesign improves the crossing while shielding and dimming other fixtures. It uses appropriate spectral and optical choices and documents the schedule. After installation, the city repeats measurements and asks residents about visibility and comfort. It monitors equipment reliability and energy use.

A successful pilot might reduce electricity consumption and skyglow while improving the particular crossing. It might also reveal an unforeseen problem, such as inadequate vertical illumination or a control schedule that causes discomfort. That would be a reason to revise the system, not to abandon the concept.

The important feature is that the city has specified competing outcomes in advance. It is no longer treating brightness as the sole measure of safety or darkness as the sole measure of environmental responsibility.

Such pilots can be scaled when their performance is understood. They can also build public trust by showing that conservation does not require ignoring nighttime users.

An effective lighting program should be able to explain not only what it turned off, but what it made work better.

## A lighting plan with six columns
A practical audit can begin with a table. Each row represents a fixture or a defined group of similar fixtures. The first column identifies the intended task: crossing, doorway, pathway, work area, sign, or decorative surface. The second records who uses it and when. The third describes the current output and distribution. The fourth lists unwanted exposure. The fifth identifies an available intervention. The sixth records how success will be measured.

The structure sounds bureaucratic, but its value is that it prevents a city from treating every lamp as interchangeable.

A crossing may require better vertical illumination of pedestrians. A decorative wall may need a schedule ending when the building closes. A residential lamp may be adequately bright on the pavement but poorly aimed into a bedroom. A fixture near a wildlife corridor may require shielding that is irrelevant to a similar lamp in a dense commercial district.

The table also reveals information gaps. The city may know the fixture's power rating but not its optical distribution. It may have complaints from residents but no nighttime site measurements. It may know where a protected habitat lies but not which lamps reach it.

Those gaps guide investigation. The objective is to make the system legible before replacing thousands of components.

An inventory can be updated as fixtures change, becoming a maintenance tool rather than an exercise performed once for a report.

### The pilot that could fail usefully

A municipality might select two comparable corridors for a lighting redesign. One receives new shielded fixtures and controls; the other retains current lighting for the initial evaluation period. Both are monitored for relevant visibility, energy use, glare complaints, and sky brightness where feasible.

This could provide better evidence than a before-and-after photograph of one street. But the design would still need to account for differences between corridors, weather, seasonal use, and the fact that lighting may influence behavior. Randomization may be possible in some settings and impractical in others.

The study should define its objectives before the changes. Is the primary goal to reduce upward emission while maintaining road visibility? Reduce unwanted bedroom exposure? Lower energy use without degrading perceived safety? A pilot can measure several outcomes, but it needs a clear way to evaluate tradeoffs.

A negative result is also informative. Perhaps sky brightness does not improve because larger private sources dominate. Perhaps glare falls but the replacement creates poor uniformity. Perhaps controls are overridden because they confuse users. Each finding points to a practical revision.

A strong program records the failure rather than selecting a photograph that makes the project look successful.

The purpose of a pilot is to learn where design changes work, not to certify that every selected technology is good.

### The private lamp the city does not own

Public streetlighting is visible in municipal budgets, but a city contains many privately controlled sources. Parking lots, warehouses, office towers, sports facilities, billboards, and homes contribute to the nighttime field.

A public retrofit can reduce emissions from city-owned lamps while private sources continue to dominate a particular horizon. That does not make the retrofit meaningless. It means the policy needs an account of the wider system.

Building codes, exterior-lighting ordinances, permitting standards, and voluntary agreements can address new private installations. Existing fixtures may be more difficult to change, especially where owners face real costs or safety obligations. Incentives and phased replacement can sometimes make improvement more practical.

Clear rules are important. A property owner should know what shielding, output, timing, and trespass conditions are expected. An ambiguous requirement to be environmentally friendly is difficult to enforce and invites disputes.

The city should also lead through its own procurement. When public fixtures demonstrate that safety and lower spill can coexist, the case for better private practices becomes easier to make.

A responsible lighting policy is not a list of lamps the government owns. It is a description of the shared environment those lamps help create.

### Spectrum belongs in context

An audit that records only warm or cool appearance may miss relevant spectral differences. Correlated color temperature is useful for describing the approximate appearance of white light, but it does not uniquely define its spectral power distribution.

A project concerned with circadian exposure or ecological effects may need more detailed spectral information. Yet not every city project has the resources to conduct elaborate biological measurements. The appropriate level of analysis depends on the sensitivity of the site and the claim being made.

A baseline approach can favor lower short-wavelength output where compatible with visual needs, combined with low levels, shielding, and controls. Sensitive habitats may warrant specialized guidance. Tasks requiring accurate color recognition may justify other choices.

The central mistake is to use one color label as a substitute for every other aspect of design. A lamp described as warm can still be excessive, badly aimed, and left on unnecessarily.

Better procurement records what is delivered to the actual task and what escapes. The color belongs within that accounting, not above it.

### The meeting where residents disagree

A public meeting about lighting can produce conflicting testimony. One resident wants a darker bedroom. Another wants a brighter path. A business owner worries that dimming will deter customers. A wildlife advocate asks for a protected corridor. An engineer explains that the current fixture sends much of its output into the wrong direction.

These perspectives are not merely preferences to be averaged. Some refer to technical requirements, some to measured environmental conditions, and some to values that the community must negotiate.

A useful process can show alternatives. Instead of asking residents to vote for brighter or darker, present designs with mapped light distributions, expected visibility, operating schedules, costs, and maintenance needs. A targeted crossing improvement may satisfy one concern while shielding a nearby lamp satisfies another.

Not every conflict will disappear. Some necessary activity creates exposure. The point is to make the tradeoffs explicit and proportionate rather than hiding them behind the default setting.

Community participation is strongest when people can see how their input changes the design and what results will be measured afterward.

### The project after the ribbon cutting

A city may announce an environmental improvement when the new fixtures are switched on. The real test begins after the celebration.

Are the controls operating as specified a year later? Have replacement fixtures preserved the intended shielding? Did complaints about glare decline or move elsewhere? Does the road remain usable during difficult weather? Is sky brightness improving under comparable measurement conditions?

Maintenance records, resident feedback, and periodic field checks help answer those questions. The results should be able to trigger changes. A lighting system that cannot be corrected after deployment is not truly adaptive, however sophisticated its original design.

The institutional challenge is that a benefit such as reduced skyglow may not have one department responsible for maintaining it. Transportation maintains roads, utilities handle equipment, parks manage habitat, and planners approve development. The lighting system crosses their boundaries.

A durable program needs ownership of the whole objective, not merely the purchase order.

### A new definition of a successful fixture

A successful outdoor lamp is not necessarily the brightest or the cheapest. It is one that delivers its required service reliably, at reasonable cost, while limiting unnecessary exposure beyond the intended area.

That definition is specific enough to guide engineering and flexible enough to accommodate different sites. A crossing and a nesting beach will have different requirements. A hospital entrance and a historic façade will have different operating needs.

The best cities will learn to make those differences deliberate. Their lighting systems will not simply accumulate output with each new development. They will become more precise as the technology improves.

Making a city less bright where brightness is not needed is not a retreat from modern infrastructure. It is a sign that the infrastructure has become mature enough to use restraint.

## A less bright city is a more deliberate city

There is no one switch that returns a metropolitan region to natural darkness. Outdoor lighting belongs to different owners and serves different activities. The light field changes with development, weather, and technology. A city that reduces waste in one sector may still receive skyglow from others.

But the problem is not beyond control. Light is unusually adjustable among environmental exposures. It can often be redirected, dimmed, scheduled, or switched off without rebuilding an entire industrial process.

The five principles of responsible outdoor lighting offer a starting point: useful, targeted, low level, controlled, and warm where appropriate. Their value lies in forcing a purpose for every lamp rather than assuming that more brightness is always better.

A city becomes less bright responsibly when it becomes better at knowing where light is necessary and where it is merely habitual.

The result may be a safer crossing, a darker bedroom, a better view of the stars, a less disrupted bat corridor, and a smaller electricity bill. The combination is not guaranteed everywhere, but it is technically possible often enough to deserve serious effort.

A mature city should not be judged by how completely it defeats the night. It should be judged by how precisely it uses light.
