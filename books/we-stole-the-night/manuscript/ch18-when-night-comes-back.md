# 18 — When Night Comes Back

The lights do not all go out at once.

A shop closes and its sign goes dark. A maintenance crew replaces an old floodlight with a shielded fixture. A park begins dimming a path after the last regular evening use. A hotel draws its beachfront blinds during nesting season. A convention center treats its windows so that migrating birds can see the barrier they once mistook for open space.

None of these changes returns the planet to an imagined prehistoric darkness. They make selected parts of the environment less wastefully bright.

That is how the night can come back: not as a universal blackout, but through the gradual recovery of places, hours, and directions that were never meant to be illuminated.

The temptation is to demand a dramatic ending. Artificial light has spread across the world; therefore the book must close with a campaign to turn everything off. But the evidence will not support that conclusion. Lighting can reduce crime under certain conditions, improve roadway visibility, enable urgent medical care, and make public space usable after sunset. The appropriate response to its environmental effects is not to deny those benefits.

The more demanding response is to distinguish light serving a purpose from light that remains because nobody has asked why it is there.

The distinction is not glamorous, but it is measurable. A city can assess illumination at a crossing, sky brightness overhead, light trespass into a bedroom, a fixture's operating hours, and exposure in a sensitive habitat. It can change the design and see whether the relevant outcomes improve.

We began with birds arriving at Chicago's McCormick Place in October 2023. Hundreds died against glass in one extraordinary migration event. Later, treatment of the building's windows sharply reduced recorded collisions. That intervention did not erase the city. It changed how a building communicated its physical boundary to animals moving through the night.

It may be the most useful image to carry forward: a problem created by human design, reduced through better human design.

## The temptation of a pure night

There is no pristine nighttime condition that a modern city can simply restore. Human beings have long used fire, torches, candles, oil, gas, and electricity to extend activity beyond sunset. Communities differ in climate, economic need, population density, and safety concerns. A rural village without reliable electricity should not be praised for its low satellite radiance as though scarcity were conservation.

Nor is natural darkness uniformly benign. Moonlight alters animal behavior. Predators and prey respond differently to light. A dangerous crossing may need more illumination, not less. A person with impaired vision may require conditions different from those comfortable to a young observer with good night adaptation.

The point of responsible lighting is not to make a moral hierarchy of people who prefer dark and people who prefer light. It is to reduce avoidable exposure while providing necessary visibility.

The five principles articulated by DarkSky International and the Illuminating Engineering Society provide a practical starting point: useful, targeted, low level, controlled, and warmer-colored where appropriate. Their strength is that they ask for purpose rather than purity.

A lamp that fulfills a necessary visual task with minimal spill is a better design than one that makes an owner feel safer by projecting light across an entire landscape. A city that improves a dangerous transit stop while reducing decorative glare elsewhere is making a coherent choice. A community that preserves a dark wildlife corridor without making pedestrian access unsafe is balancing legitimate users.

This is the kind of progress that rarely produces a heroic photograph. It is too local and technical to fit the usual story of environmental victory. But its consequences can be real.

## What the evidence actually says

The scientific record is broader and more varied than a single claim that artificial light is harmful.

The 2016 world atlas estimated that most people lived under light-polluted skies and that the Milky Way had disappeared from view for a substantial share of humanity. The 2023 citizen-science analysis inferred a rapid rise in perceived sky brightness over its 2011–2022 observation period, while acknowledging the limitations of volunteer observations and the differences between ground and satellite measures.

Field experiments linked nighttime illumination to reduced nocturnal pollinator visits in Swiss meadows. Other studies identified changed activity in bats, altered fish and zooplankton distributions in lakes, and disrupted reproductive timing in certain coral species. A 2026 bird meta-analysis found consistent physiological and behavioral responses across studies, with variation in life-history outcomes.

Human biology provides another line of evidence. Circadian rhythms are real physiological processes, and light is an important environmental cue. The IARC evaluation classified night shift work as probably carcinogenic under a defined evidence framework, but that occupational classification cannot be assigned casually to every lamp or screen.

Public safety research adds a necessary complication. A randomized lighting intervention in New York public-housing developments reduced certain nighttime outdoor crimes under its conditions. A separate observational study of lighting reductions in England and Wales found little evidence of broad aggregate increases in crime or nighttime road collisions under the strategies examined. The results are compatible when their contexts and designs are respected.

This is not a collection of contradictory facts waiting for one slogan to organize them. It is a picture of an environmental intervention whose effects depend on the place, purpose, species, exposure, and alternative designs.

A serious policy can accommodate that complexity. A lazy one will choose only the results that support its preferred level of brightness.

## The modest experiment a town can run

A small town does not need to solve global light pollution before improving its own nighttime environment. It can begin by asking a practical question: where does our outdoor light go, and what work does it perform?

The town might identify a corridor where residents complain of glare and where fixtures send substantial light upward. It can document existing conditions, including energy consumption, visual performance, and sky brightness. It can choose a redesign that preserves necessary roadway visibility while improving shielding and operating schedules.

After installation, it can measure again. Did residents see hazards more clearly? Did glare decline? Were electricity and maintenance costs affected? Did the sky become darker under comparable observation conditions? If a nearby sensitive habitat was part of the objective, did exposure decrease in a way that matters for that habitat?

The experiment may not produce a perfect result. A dimming schedule could inconvenience early commuters. A lamp could be mis-aimed. A particular street might need more vertical illumination at a crossing. Those are engineering findings, not reasons to abandon restraint.

The town can revise its design and keep the evidence. The project becomes a learning process instead of an ideological contest.

This method is transferable. A beachfront resort can evaluate source visibility from nesting sand. A commercial building can measure how much light escapes after closing. A hospital can distinguish patient rest areas from critical task lighting. A regional authority can coordinate limits on upward emission around an observatory.

The common principle is to design for the actual task and inspect the unintended reach.

## The wrong question about technology

We often ask whether a new technology is good or bad for the environment. LEDs make that question especially misleading. They have delivered enormous gains in energy efficiency and control. They can also support the expansion of illumination if lower cost encourages more fixtures and longer operation.

The technology does not determine the outcome by itself. Optical design, procurement, operating practices, and local regulation shape how the devices are used.

The same is true of sensors and adaptive controls. A motion detector can reduce unnecessary operating time. It can also fail, produce abrupt changes, or be bypassed because maintenance is inconvenient. An automated system can optimize illumination for measured occupancy while ignoring ecological activity that its sensors do not detect.

The most important technological question is therefore not whether we possess smarter lamps. It is whether we know what objective the lamps should serve.

A city can automate a bad lighting policy. It can also use ordinary, reliable controls to implement a good one. Software does not replace judgment about safety, ecology, comfort, and public access.

The future of responsible lighting is likely to involve better materials, better optics, better controls, and better measurement. But those improvements matter only when they are attached to clear purposes and credible evaluation.

## A night for the living

The night belongs to people who need to work and travel after sunset. It belongs to children trying to sleep, residents looking out of windows, and observers looking for stars. It is also habitat for insects, bats, birds, aquatic organisms, and other life whose use of darkness predates our urban infrastructure.

These interests do not always align. A lamp can help one and disturb another. That is not a reason for despair. It is the normal condition of engineering in a shared environment.

The first step is to stop pretending that a fixture's effect ends at the surface it was purchased to illuminate. Light travels. It changes visual backgrounds, timing signals, movement, and ecological relationships. Its benefits and costs can occur in different places.

The second step is to stop treating brightness as a universal sign of progress. A well-lit crossing can be progress. So can a darker shoreline, a bedroom free from glare, or a protected observatory horizon. Their value comes from the function they serve, not from their place on a brightness scale.

The third step is to measure the consequences rather than rely on appearance. A beautiful night photograph does not prove ecological recovery. A lower electricity bill does not guarantee less skyglow. A bright street does not automatically mean fewer crimes. A dark street does not automatically mean safety has been sacrificed.

These are questions for evidence.

## A restoration that can be measured
Imagine a small town announcing that it will make its night sky darker. The announcement alone tells us almost nothing about whether the project will work. The town needs a baseline, an intervention, and a definition of success.

It might begin by identifying sources that send substantial light upward or into neighboring properties. The public works department can examine fixture optics and operating hours. Residents can report glare and bedroom intrusion. Observers can record the sky under repeated comparable conditions. A local environmental group may identify sensitive habitats where exposure has a plausible biological effect.

The next step is choosing which changes to make. A road crossing may require better directed illumination rather than less. A commercial façade may be darkened after closing. A parking lot may be shielded to prevent spill into nearby houses and trees. The goal is a collection of targeted improvements, not a ceremonial reduction in total lamp count.

The town should measure outcomes after installation. Did useful roadway visibility remain adequate? Did complaints about glare decline? Did the sky become darker under comparable conditions? Did electricity consumption change? Where ecological outcomes are claimed, was there relevant monitoring?

Some results may disappoint. A new shield may improve a neighbor's window without producing a detectable change in regional skyglow. That is still a real local benefit. A dramatic reduction in energy use may produce little astronomical improvement if other sources dominate. The report should distinguish these results rather than merge them into one environmental success score.

The night can return in degrees, places, and hours. The measurement should reflect that.

### The first dark hour

A town might discover that one of its simplest opportunities is time. A decorative building façade is illuminated late after most people have left. A sign remains bright all night even though the business is closed. A sports facility keeps some exterior fixtures operating long after an event.

A scheduled reduction may preserve the intended early-evening service while limiting exposure during low-use hours. The change can save electricity and reduce light emitted into the surroundings.

But the schedule should be grounded in actual use. A hospital entrance cannot operate like a closed office. A transit stop may remain important through the night. A coastal nesting beach may be sensitive during hours that overlap with tourist activity. A wildlife corridor may be used most intensively at dusk rather than midnight.

The first dark hour is therefore not one universal time. It is the hour when a particular light's purpose ends or can be served by a lower level.

Modern controls make timing easier, but maintenance remains essential. A timer that fails, an override never reset, or a control policy not understood by staff can erase the benefit. The system needs to be simple enough to operate reliably.

A small durable change can be more valuable than an ambitious automated scheme that does not survive its first year.

### The stars do not return equally

Reducing light pollution in a dense urban region may make the sky somewhat darker without restoring the Milky Way. A rural town close to the threshold of good astronomical visibility may preserve or recover far more visible structure from a smaller change.

This inequality is a feature of the physical problem. Skyglow accumulates from many sources, and atmospheric conditions vary. It also raises a question of priorities. Should resources focus on protecting dark regions where a modest intervention preserves a rare experience, or on improving urban conditions where many people are exposed?

Both can be legitimate. Protecting a dark-sky park or observatory serves scientific and cultural purposes. Reducing glare in dense neighborhoods can improve daily comfort for residents who may never travel to that park. A city can have meaningful environmental goals without promising a pristine sky over every district.

The relevant benefit should be described accurately. A successful urban retrofit may reduce light trespass and save energy even when astronomy remains difficult. A regional observatory protection program may preserve scientific performance without changing most residents' bedrooms.

One environmental slogan cannot substitute for the distinct outcomes.

The best approach is to retain several goals and measure each on its own terms.

### The inconvenient question of more light

A book called *We Stole the Night* must be willing to recommend a new lamp in some locations. That is not an exception that undermines the argument. It is the consequence of taking the evidence seriously.

If a pedestrian crossing is inadequately lit and better illumination can improve hazard recognition, the design should address that need. If a public-housing area suffers from inadequate nighttime visibility and a well-designed intervention can reduce crime or expand safe public use, the evidence deserves attention. If emergency services need light to operate, the lighting is not waste merely because it is bright.

The question is whether the benefit requires the particular output chosen. A crossing may be improved with carefully directed light that produces less total spill than a broad floodlight. A transit route may need continuous task lighting while nearby decorative sources can dim.

The goal is not a world with fewer lamps at any price. It is a world where lighting is justified by its function and designed to minimize unnecessary consequences.

This is how environmental restraint can coexist with human development. We are not required to freeze the nighttime economy to protect important parts of darkness.

A more precise second sun is a more useful one.

### The species that might not recover

The physical darkness around a lamp can often be restored quickly by switching it off. Ecological recovery is not always as immediate.

A population may have been altered by multiple pressures. Habitat fragmentation, warming, chemicals, invasive species, and other changes can continue even after nighttime illumination is reduced. A moth network may not return to its former structure if the plants or insects are gone. A turtle beach may still face coastal development and climate stress. A bird-friendly building cannot eliminate every hazard of migration.

This is a reason to avoid promising that darkness alone will repair ecosystems. It is not a reason to ignore a controllable exposure.

A responsible restoration project identifies which mechanism it seeks to address and monitors the relevant biological response where feasible. Reduced light may be one useful part of a larger conservation effort. The result should be reported within that context.

There is also a knowledge gap. Some nocturnal processes are poorly studied, and the absence of a measured recovery may reflect limited monitoring. Researchers should avoid interpreting every uncertain outcome as success or failure.

The most honest claim is that lowering unnecessary exposure can remove an identified environmental pressure. What follows depends on the ecology of the place.

### What a generation inherits

The night changes slowly enough that the loss can become normal. A child growing up in a bright city may never see the Milky Way and therefore may not feel that anything has vanished. An older resident may remember stars that are no longer visible. Both are describing their actual experience of the same place at different times.

This creates a peculiar form of environmental amnesia. Each generation treats the condition it encounters as the ordinary starting point. A gradual loss of sky visibility can occur without a common date on which the public decided to accept it.

Restoration changes the story. When a community reduces skyglow, new observers may discover a feature that older residents remember. The improvement can become tangible through shared observations, school programs, or simply looking up on a clear night.

There is value in that experience even when it cannot be assigned a precise financial return. The sky has shaped navigation, science, art, calendars, and human curiosity. Recovering access to part of it is a legitimate cultural objective.

It should not become an elitist one. People who need safe streets and electricity have no lesser claim on progress. A responsible plan uses better technology to deliver both utility and a more usable nighttime environment.

The generation that inherited excess brightness can also inherit the means to control it.

### An ordinary final decision

At the beginning of the book, a building on Chicago's lakefront became the scene of a mass bird collision. Its windows and illumination met an ancient migration route under dangerous weather conditions. The later window treatment offered a practical example of changing the built environment to reduce a measurable hazard.

That is more useful than ending with a demand for repentance. The people operating buildings and cities can make different decisions when the evidence becomes visible. Some require investment. Others require a new schedule, a shield, or the willingness to question an inherited specification.

The same principle reaches across the subjects of this book: human circadian timing, insects that pollinate after sunset, aquatic animals using different depths at night, sea turtles following an ocean horizon, bats moving between habitats, residents affected by glare, and telescopes struggling against a bright background.

Their responses differ. They should not be flattened into one claim that every photon is harmful. The common condition is that human lighting reaches beyond its intended user.

A mature lighting culture would make that reach part of the design decision. It would ask what the light is for, where it goes, when it is needed, and who else receives it.

The night will not return everywhere in the same way. But it can return to a window, a corridor, a beach, a lake, or part of the sky.

It can return because a particular light no longer has a reason to be there.

## An answer that can survive disagreement

A neighbor who wants a brighter walkway and an astronomer who wants a darker horizon do not need to agree on every value to agree on a measurement. They can ask where the illumination falls, how well a person can see a hazard, what a fixture contributes to skyglow, and how much output is wasted outside its intended task.

The results will not dictate every decision. A community still has to decide which benefits deserve priority and which costs it can reasonably accept. But the disagreement becomes more precise when it is about an observable trade rather than a symbol.

There is no universal brightness that makes a society good. There is a responsibility to explain why an environmental exposure is necessary and to reduce the part that serves no legitimate purpose. Some lights deserve to be brighter, others dimmer, and many should simply point somewhere else.

This principle is durable because it does not depend on one disputed health statistic or one species' response. It can be applied whenever a light's useful work and unwanted reach can be separated. The remaining challenge is institutional: someone must own the question after the fixture has been installed.

## The stars were there all along

A person returning to a dark place after years in a city can experience a startling change. The sky seems fuller than memory allowed. Faint stars and the Milky Way become visible under suitable conditions. The universe did not suddenly grow more crowded. The background against which it was seen became darker.

That is a useful image because it describes a reversible part of the problem. Some environmental changes require generations of repair. Unwanted light can sometimes be reduced tonight by aiming, dimming, or switching off a source.

Not every lost ecological effect will reverse so quickly. Populations can be stressed by several pressures, and a habitat may need more than darkness to recover. The science is still developing. But the physical possibility of reducing unnecessary illumination is unusually direct.

This should make the issue more hopeful, not less serious. We are not confronting an unavoidable byproduct of human existence. We are confronting a vast number of design decisions, many of which can be improved.

The Chicago building did not need to disappear for bird collisions to fall. Its glass needed to become visible. The street did not need to vanish for its lighting to be more carefully directed. The beach did not need to close for artificial sources to be managed during turtle nesting season. A city need not stop living at night to recover part of its sky.

The most important invention may no longer be a brighter lamp. It may be the habit of asking whether a lamp should be bright at all.

We stole the night in millions of small decisions that made sense to us at the time. We can return some of it through decisions that take the rest of the living world into account.

There is no need for a ceremonial blackout. On an ordinary evening, somewhere, a light can simply stop shining where it was never needed.
