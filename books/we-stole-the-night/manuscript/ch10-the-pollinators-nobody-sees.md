# 10 — The Pollinators Nobody Sees

The flower does not close its business when the sun goes down.

A person walking through a garden in daylight sees bees, butterflies, and other familiar visitors. The scene encourages a convenient assumption: pollination is a daytime service. The insects working after sunset are less conspicuous, partly because we are less likely to look for them.

Moths and other nocturnal animals visit flowers, carry pollen, and participate in ecological networks that can extend beyond the plant's nighttime life. Their work is not necessarily interchangeable with the work of daytime pollinators. The timing of visits, the species that participate, and their connections with plants can differ.

In 2017, Eva Knop and colleagues published a field experiment in *Nature* titled [*Artificial light at night as a new threat to pollination*](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature23288). The researchers studied plant-pollinator communities in Swiss meadows exposed to artificial nighttime lighting. In illuminated plots, nocturnal visits to flowers were reduced by 62 percent compared with dark plots. For a focal plant, fruit set was reduced by 13 percent despite the presence of daytime pollination.

These numbers are powerful because they connect behavior to an ecological function. They are also not universal estimates for all insects, plants, or lighting technologies. The experiment involved particular communities, conditions, and exposures. It demonstrated a mechanism worth investigating more widely, not that every lamp reduces global food production by 13 percent.

The distinction is important. One of the most common ways to exaggerate environmental research is to take a well-designed local experiment and convert its result into a planetary average. The original study deserves better than that.

Its central lesson is already consequential: when we change the night, we can change a biological exchange that humans rarely observe.

## A network after closing

Pollination is often introduced as a relationship between a flower and an insect. In reality, plant-pollinator communities form networks. A single insect species may visit several plants; a plant may receive visits from many insects; some interactions occur during the day and others at night.

The network structure matters because an effect on one group can influence the rest. If artificial light reduces nocturnal visits to a plant, its reproductive success may change. That can affect the plant's abundance over time and potentially the resources available to other organisms. The consequences may extend beyond the illuminated hour.

The Knop study did more than count moths under lamps. It analyzed nocturnal interaction networks and connected nighttime disruption with a measured change in fruit set for a particular plant. It also examined the relationship between daytime and nighttime pollination networks, suggesting that disruption of one part can propagate into the larger system.

The conclusion should be stated carefully. A field experiment can demonstrate a change under its exposure conditions; the long-term population consequences of that change depend on many additional factors. Climate, land use, pesticides, invasive species, pathogens, habitat fragmentation, and other pressures affect pollinators. Light is one driver interacting with them, not a substitute explanation for every decline.

The more interesting question is why we failed to notice the service in the first place. Nocturnal pollination takes place when fewer people are observing flowers. The activity can be substantial without being culturally visible. Its invisibility makes it easy to assume that a garden's ecological work ends at dusk.

Artificial light creates a special kind of error here: it can make the garden more visible to us while making some of its nighttime relationships function differently.

The flower has not changed its biological requirements because we installed a lamp. The insects have encountered a new environment in which light alters movement, attraction, avoidance, timing, or exposure to predators.

## The lamp as a competing flower

Some nocturnal insects are attracted to artificial lights. A streetlamp can gather moths and other flying insects that would otherwise be distributed across the landscape. The familiar image of insects circling a bulb is not proof that all such attraction is uniform or that insects are literally mistaking lamps for flowers.

Light-oriented behavior is complex. Different insects respond to wavelengths, intensity, direction, and environmental context. Some may be drawn toward bright sources; others avoid them. Flight orientation mechanisms can become disrupted near artificial illumination. The consequences may include altered feeding, mating, movement, and vulnerability to predators.

The important point is that a lamp is not ecologically neutral merely because it does not directly touch an animal. It can change where an insect spends time.

A moth diverted from visiting a flower may miss a feeding opportunity. A congregation of insects near a light may provide prey for certain bats and spiders. The total ecological result depends on which species are attracted, which avoid the light, and how the altered behavior affects reproduction and survival.

Some animals can benefit from the new concentration of prey, at least locally. That makes the effect harder to describe as a simple decline in every component of the ecosystem. A light may create winners and losers within the same food web.

The question is whether those new relationships maintain the functions of the older system. A bat feeding around a lamp may be thriving while another bat species avoids the same area. A moth might be present in the vicinity yet no longer visiting flowers at the rate it otherwise would. An insect count near a lamp could be high even as the surrounding pollination network becomes less effective.

This is why a study of insect abundance alone is insufficient. We need to know what the insects are doing.

## What time is insect time?

The night is not one uniform ecological interval. Activity can vary sharply between dusk, the early evening, midnight, the hours before dawn, and dawn itself. Different insects occupy different temporal niches. A lighting change that looks modest when averaged across the entire night may intersect with a peak period for a particular species.

A 2026 paper in *Communications Biology*, [*Citizen science reveals temporally structured insect attraction and mitigation efficacy under artificial light at night*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-026-10979-2), investigated nighttime insect attraction along a lakeside road. The researchers compared conventional high-pressure sodium lighting with tailored and shielded LED road lights, using observations across five nightly intervals and complementary traps.

They found that insect attraction varied through the night, with strong activity around late evening and midnight in the study conditions. The tailored and shielded LED fixtures reduced total insect attraction, particularly during high-activity periods, but their benefits were not identical at every time of night. Taxonomic composition also varied.

The result is valuable because it challenges a design habit: treating nighttime exposure as a single average. A lamp used at 10 p.m. may affect a different biological community from the same lamp used at 4 a.m., even when brightness and location remain unchanged.

It also suggests that timed controls could achieve environmental benefits without eliminating illumination whenever people need it. If a road or facility has low human activity during a period of high insect sensitivity, dimming may reduce exposure at a relatively low social cost. But the local evidence matters. The timing of insect activity will vary by climate, season, habitat, and species.

The experiment does not establish one universal best dimming time for all roads. It demonstrates why time belongs in the specification.

A city that knows only the lamp's annual operating hours may be missing the most important detail: which hours overlap with the biological processes nearby.

## Why a bright garden can be a poorer garden

A garden illuminated for human enjoyment can look spectacular. Flowers remain visible after sunset, paths become accessible, and social life continues. The visual effect can be so attractive that it is hard to imagine the garden functioning less well.

But a landscape's appearance to people is not the same as its ecological performance. The Knop experiment showed that illuminated plant-pollinator communities can receive fewer nocturnal visits and that a focal plant's fruit set can decline under the tested conditions. The physical beauty of a brightly lit flower bed cannot tell us whether pollination is proceeding normally.

There is a design lesson that does not require abolishing nighttime gardens. Different zones can serve different purposes. A gathering area may need illumination for people; a pollinator habitat may benefit from reduced direct exposure. Shielded, low-level, timed lighting can reduce spill while preserving access. A garden may be designed so that pathways are visible without bathing every flowering plant in decorative light.

The outcome should be evaluated rather than assumed. A particular spectral change may alter which insects are attracted. A shield may reduce exposure in one part of the garden but not another. A dimming schedule may be effective only during certain activity periods.

The right question is not whether the garden appears natural. It is whether the design supports the organisms and interactions the owner says they value.

A school, park, or public garden could make this visible by monitoring night pollinators alongside daytime species. Such observations require care and appropriate methods, but they can reveal an otherwise invisible part of the place's ecology. Education can change the sense that every dark corner is a defect waiting for a lamp.

The darkness itself may be supporting work.

## The economic invisibility of a moth

Pollination has economic importance, but the value of nighttime pollination is difficult to compress into a single price. The ecological connections are species-specific, spatially complex, and shaped by more than one environmental pressure. A plant's reproductive success can matter to animals and habitats beyond agricultural output.

It would be tempting to assign a dramatic dollar amount to every moth diverted by streetlighting. That would create a compelling headline and a weak scientific claim. The actual effect of an illuminated road depends on local species, plants, alternative habitats, and the structure of interactions.

The more defensible economic observation is about omitted costs. A lighting project is commonly assessed for capital expenditure, electricity, maintenance, and human safety. Pollination disruption may be outside the account entirely. Its absence from the spreadsheet does not establish that the cost is zero.

Sometimes the environmental benefit of reducing spill is low cost. A shielded fixture or timer may preserve the intended lighting service while reducing exposure. In other cases, a meaningful change may require redesign and negotiation among users.

The task is to make the ecological variable visible in the decision, even when it cannot be valued perfectly in money.

This is similar to the broader problem of insect decline. A population can be affected by multiple pressures, and researchers may struggle to assign a precise share of the decline to one cause. That uncertainty does not justify ignoring a controllable exposure whose local effects have been demonstrated. It does justify avoiding broad numerical claims unsupported by the evidence.

An engineer can say, under these conditions, shielded lighting reduces insect attraction. A conservation planner can say, this habitat contains important nocturnal pollination interactions. A business owner can say, this lamp is necessary for safe movement. The project can then search for a design that respects all three facts.

## Timing as a form of habitat

Habitat is usually imagined spatially: a forest, meadow, wetland, or shoreline. But for many organisms, time is part of habitat. A plant may open its flowers in the evening. An insect may forage during a particular interval. A predator may hunt when moonlight makes prey visible. The environment's daily schedule can be as important as its physical boundaries.

Artificial light changes temporal habitat by making a place brighter during periods when it would otherwise be dark. The exposure may move behavior into different hours, prolong activity, or prevent organisms from using a preferred period.

A strong conservation policy could recognize this by managing light seasonally and hourly. There is no reason to keep the same lighting level throughout a park if human use and ecological sensitivity both change predictably. A timed reduction can be more targeted than a permanent ban.

But timing also introduces maintenance and compliance problems. Clocks must be accurate, controls must work, and the system must accommodate safety needs. A design that depends on a complicated schedule but fails frequently may provide little ecological benefit. Simplicity and reliability are part of responsibility.

The 2026 insect study's significance lies partly in its reminder that ecological timing must be measured. An arbitrary midnight cutoff may align poorly with insect activity in one region. A local study can identify when exposure is most consequential.

This is not a universal recipe, but it is a better question than whether the lamp is on or off for the whole night.

## The experiment behind a percentage
The most memorable figure from the 2017 Swiss meadow study is the 62 percent reduction in nocturnal flower visits in illuminated plots. Before that number becomes an argument for a particular city ordinance, it needs to be read as an experimental result with a denominator.

The researchers were comparing visits under specified lighting conditions in field communities they could monitor. They were not surveying every moth, every flowering plant, and every lamp. Their outcome was the frequency of observed nocturnal interactions. The focal plant's fruit-set response provided a second kind of evidence, suggesting that the disruption could reach a reproductive function rather than remaining a change in insect movement.

The strength of the study came from connecting these levels. A difference in visits alone might be compensated by daytime pollinators. A difference in fruit set suggests the network's total service was affected under the conditions tested. Yet the 13 percent figure belongs to the focal plant and experimental setting. It cannot be applied as a multiplier to every crop or wildflower illuminated after sunset.

That distinction matters because pollination is not a generic commodity. The timing of pollen availability, the shape of a flower, insect mouthparts, the amount of compatible pollen carried, and the behavior of other visitors all affect reproductive outcomes. Ten visits from one insect species may contribute differently from ten visits by another.

A future study testing a different lamp should therefore measure more than how many insects approach it. The strongest design would track visits to relevant plants, pollen transfer or reproductive outcomes where feasible, and the composition of the nocturnal community. Counts of insects trapped near a light are useful for one question and insufficient for another.

A policy justified by nocturnal pollination should aim to preserve the actual exchange, not merely produce an impressive photograph of moths avoiding a fixture.

### A bright trap and a dark flower

Consider a roadside meadow separated from a lamp by a few meters of grass. Some insects flying toward the illumination may have been about to visit flowers. Others may have come from elsewhere or may not have been feeding at all. The lamp can change the distribution of activity without changing the total number of insects in the wider landscape.

This makes a simple trap count ambiguous. Catching more insects near a lamp can indicate attraction, but not necessarily a net increase in insect abundance. Finding fewer insects in an adjacent dark patch could indicate redistribution rather than mortality. The ecological effect depends on where the insects would otherwise have gone and what they would have done.

One way to investigate is to combine methods: direct flower-visit observations, light traps used with known limitations, surveys at matched distances from fixtures, and repeated sampling across nights and seasons. The question of pollination requires information about insects interacting with plants, not only insects located near light.

A robust study would also consider the biological time of night. A flower may offer nectar or present receptive structures during a relatively narrow period. A lamp that disrupts insect movement during that window could matter more than one operating during a period of low activity, even if both emit the same total amount of light.

That is why the 2026 lakeside insect-attraction study's timing results are useful beyond its particular location. The study did not measure every pollination network, but it demonstrated how the same artificial source can have different biological effects across a night. A fixed annual energy total cannot reveal that pattern.

If a municipal park or garden wants to reduce ecological disruption, it should ask when local pollinators are active and which areas contain plants depending on their visits. That is a more useful question than asking for one universal insect-safe lamp.

### The habitat that pays twice

A moth may encounter artificial light at several stages of its life. Adult behavior is the most visible around lamps, but changes in feeding and mating can affect reproduction, while developmental conditions and habitat resources shape the population available to pollinate next season.

The effect can also be indirect. An illuminated area may favor a predator that feeds on insects gathered at the lamp. The moths that escape may still lose time they would have spent feeding. A shift in where insects fly can change which plants receive visits. Any one of these mechanisms could matter, and they need not all operate under the same light source.

There is a danger of making this web of possibilities sound like a list of established harms. Many proposed pathways remain context-dependent, and their net consequences are often difficult to quantify. The scientific task is to measure them, not to assume that every plausible link carries a large negative effect.

A useful experiment might compare a shielded path lamp with an unshielded one while holding useful human illumination constant. It could measure insect movement and nocturnal flower visits on both sides of the path, with dark control areas, over a season. If the shield reduces spill and the ecological interaction changes in the predicted direction, the design has earned evidence for a local benefit.

The control must be genuine. If the treated site has different plants or weather from the control, a difference in moth activity may not be attributable to lighting. Randomized or crossed lighting schedules, where feasible and safe, can strengthen the comparison.

This is what makes the policy problem tractable. The relevant exposure can often be changed. The experimental outcome can be recorded. The recommendation need not wait for a complete theory of every insect in the landscape.

### The farmer and the conservationist

Pollination becomes politically interesting when a function valued for its ecological role intersects with the economics of land use. A farmer may want reliable crop pollination, pest management, safe machinery operation, and security. A conservationist may want nocturnal insects to retain feeding and breeding habitat. A village may want an illuminated path connecting fields and homes.

These interests do not have to be presented as natural enemies. A targeted lamp can serve a farm gate without illuminating an entire flowering hedgerow. A scheduled work light can support a necessary operation while remaining off during unused hours. A protected dark corridor can preserve insect movement between habitats.

But some conflicts remain. Harvesting or processing may legitimately require light during a biologically sensitive time. A business owner may bear a real cost from changing operations, while the ecological benefit is shared. A successful policy needs to acknowledge those costs and seek proportionate interventions.

The ecological outcome should also be named honestly. A dark hedgerow is not guaranteed to produce higher crop yields. Pollinator networks vary, and crop reproduction can depend on many species and conditions. The argument for reducing spill can rest on preserving a demonstrated ecological process without promising a precise agricultural return at every site.

That restraint matters commercially as well as scientifically. Projects are easier to maintain when their claims survive inspection. A landowner who is promised a large yield increase from switching off lights may abandon the effort when the increase does not appear. A landowner shown the measured reduction in exposure and the biological mechanism has a clearer reason to continue.

### An invisible shift in the food supply

The nocturnal pollinator is easy to overlook because the public image of pollination is a bee in daylight. That image can make conservation priorities overly narrow. Protecting flowering habitat is important, but the habitat's light environment is another variable affecting some of the species using it.

There is a useful lesson here for environmental accounting: count interactions, not only objects. A garden can contain the same number of flower species after installation of lighting while the visits between plants and insects change. A pond can contain the same total plankton density while the organisms occupy different depths. A city can retain its trees and still interrupt a bat's route between them with illumination.

Conventional planning often inventories things that stay in place. The ecology of nighttime light is about relationships that move across time. They are harder to observe and easier to erase from a budget.

A genuinely responsible garden would evaluate its appearance and its function. People need not abandon nighttime enjoyment. They can illuminate routes and gathering areas with directed, controlled light and leave sensitive patches dark. The ecological benefit will vary, but the effort begins from the right question.

When the visitors are mostly invisible to us, we should be slower to infer that the flowers have no customers.

## A flower with no audience

The nighttime pollinator does not require a human observer to make its work real. That may be the most important conceptual shift in the chapter. We have built our lighting environment around what people can see and do, then judged its success partly by how appealing the scene looks to people.

A meadow can look empty after sunset while remaining biologically busy. A lamp can make it easier for us to admire flowers while changing the visits those flowers receive. The contradiction is not obvious until someone measures it.

Knop and colleagues did that in a specific Swiss field experiment. Later work adds more detail about timing, species composition, and design interventions. Together the studies make a case for treating nocturnal pollination as an ecological function that deserves protection, without pretending that every illuminated site has the same consequences.

The practical response is selective light: enough for the human task, aimed where required, managed at the times it is needed, and kept away from sensitive habitat where feasible.

We do not need to see a moth at work to acknowledge that the work is happening. We need enough evidence to stop designing as though the dark part of a garden is unoccupied.
