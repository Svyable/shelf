# 5 — The Cheap Light Paradox

The LED was supposed to make lighting less expensive. It did.

That statement deserves to be taken seriously before the next one: the night sky can grow brighter even as the devices illuminating it become more efficient.

A light-emitting diode converts electrical energy into visible radiation through a semiconductor process. Practical LED lighting has achieved major advances in efficacy, optical control, lifetime, and adaptability. Compared with older incandescent technology, LEDs can deliver similar useful light for a fraction of the electricity. They also allow engineers to direct light more precisely and change output through electronic control.

The [International Energy Agency's 2026 analysis](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/the-next-wave-of-led-lighting-smarter-circular-and-more-efficient) described substantial global electricity savings from efficient lighting. It estimated that, if lamp efficacies and market shares had remained at earlier levels, annual indoor lighting electricity consumption in residential and service buildings would be dramatically higher. Its counterfactual is a modeling estimate of avoided demand, not an invoice from one universal grid.

The energy benefit is real. It should not be minimized because someone dislikes bright modern streetlights. An efficient lamp can lower operating expenses, reduce energy demand, and allow better control of illumination. Where communities still lack reliable light, the technology can expand useful services with lower resource requirements.

Yet lower cost also makes more lighting affordable. A property owner who once illuminated one doorway may now illuminate the whole façade. A city that saves money during a streetlight retrofit may install additional fixtures, operate them longer, or specify greater brightness. The same device that makes responsible lighting technically easier can make indiscriminate lighting economically easier.

This is the cheap-light paradox. We improved the efficiency of producing illumination without automatically improving the efficiency of *using* illumination.

The difference is the plot of the next hundred years.

## What a lumen can buy

The efficiency of a lamp is commonly expressed as lumens per watt: the amount of luminous output delivered for the electrical power consumed. The comparison is useful because electricity bills are based on energy, not on how beautifully a lamp looks.

But the figure is not a guarantee that a fixture will illuminate the intended target efficiently. A light source can generate many lumens and waste much of them through poor optics, aiming, or placement. An outdoor installation has to account for where the light falls.

The [U.S. Department of Energy's exterior-lighting guidance](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-exterior-lighting) distinguishes luminaire efficacy from product category and notes that advanced controls can add savings beyond merely replacing the source. It includes different efficiency requirements for roadway, garage, canopy, wall-mounted, and other exterior fixtures. That specificity is important. There is no single ideal lamp for every public-space task.

An LED streetlight can be designed to minimize upward emission and reduce waste outside the roadway. An LED floodlight can be aimed badly enough to create severe glare and spill. The semiconductor does not determine which result occurs. Its package, optics, mounting, controls, and use do.

The [DOE's roadway lighting research](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/ssl/roadway-lighting-research) emphasizes the opportunity to achieve required roadway light levels with less generated light because LED optics can be more controllable. The research also concerns visibility, safety, energy, and human health. Its framing is not that every street needs to be brighter. It is that the right output can be delivered with less waste if the application is understood.

There is a commercial lesson in this. A lighting company can sell a number of watts saved because the utility bill makes the gain visible. It is harder to sell reduced skyglow, better sleep conditions, or less disrupted insect behavior because those benefits may appear outside the customer's property and outside a conventional accounting period.

Procurement tends to reward what can be measured and purchased easily. A more complete specification would ask not only how much power the fixture consumes, but how much useful visibility it produces without excessive glare, spill, or biological exposure.

When that broader goal is stated clearly, an efficient LED becomes a tool for improvement rather than an excuse for expansion.

## The rebound that is not a law

Economists use the term rebound effect for situations in which greater efficiency reduces the effective cost of a service and encourages additional consumption of it. If illumination becomes cheaper, demand for illumination may increase. In extreme cases, increased use can offset some or all of the energy savings from a more efficient technology.

It is tempting to invoke Jevons' paradox every time a city installs LEDs and the sky appears brighter. The historical idea is important, but the modern claim needs evidence about actual use. It is not a law that every efficiency gain will be consumed away. A town may replace lamps at equal or lower output, install shielding and controls, and realize both energy savings and reduced skyglow. Another may use the savings to light many additional surfaces. Their outcomes differ because of decisions, not the mere presence of LEDs.

A 2022 [review of environmental impacts of artificial light at night](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-environ-112420-014438) discussed rebound effects and the difficulty of capturing ecological and astronomical consequences in conventional life-cycle assessments. The paper's value is the distinction between energy use and wider environmental impact. A product can perform well on lifetime electricity consumption while creating substantial exposure if installed indiscriminately.

The 2023 Kyba study observed declining star visibility over the prior decade and discussed spectral change, emission geometry, and possible rebound as part of the interpretation. It did not prove that rebound was the sole cause of the entire trend. A clean causal study would need to know what fixtures were replaced, what new fixtures were installed, how operating hours changed, and how the relevant sky responded.

Suppose a municipality claims that its LED conversion reduced its streetlighting energy use by half. That may be an excellent result. But if the same project doubles the illuminated area or adds blue-rich glare, the experience of the night may become worse for some people or species even while the municipal utility bill falls.

The two outcomes are not contradictory. The energy metric and the light-exposure metric describe different things.

The solution is not to abandon efficiency. It is to stop allowing efficiency to be the entire definition of success.

## The wrong color of progress

Many older streetlights produced a characteristically amber appearance. High-pressure sodium lamps, in particular, became familiar across roads and parking areas. LED replacements often emit white light with a different spectral distribution. White light can improve color discrimination and sometimes visibility under particular conditions. It can also increase shorter-wavelength output relative to some previous sources, with implications for skyglow, circadian exposure, and wildlife.

The word *white* conceals a range of spectra. Many white LEDs combine a blue-emitting semiconductor with a phosphor that converts part of the emission into longer wavelengths. Products can be engineered for warmer or cooler appearance, different color rendering, and different spectral profiles.

The amount of short-wavelength light matters for several reasons. Human circadian photoreception involves melanopsin-containing retinal cells that respond strongly to portions of the blue-cyan region. Scattering of light in the atmosphere is wavelength dependent. Some animals respond differently to different parts of the spectrum. An astronomical instrument may have its own bandpass and sensitivity.

But spectrum cannot be evaluated independently of intensity, direction, and timing. A warm lamp left on at high output all night can create greater unwanted exposure than a cooler lamp used briefly and shielded well. A narrow-band amber light can still misdirect wildlife if placed in the wrong location. A white LED may be necessary for a task requiring accurate color identification, and its impact can sometimes be managed through aiming and dimming.

One of the most persistent mistakes in the debate is to turn correlated color temperature into a complete ecological rating. A lamp labeled 2700 K is often preferable to a 5000 K product for reducing short-wavelength content, but CCT does not uniquely specify a spectral power distribution. Two lamps with the same CCT can differ in important ways. Total biological exposure remains a function of spectrum, level, timing, and geometry.

A meaningful procurement system could specify both human visual performance and environmental exposure limits, with task-specific exceptions. That would be more defensible than a universal statement that one color is safe and another is toxic.

Engineering is often the discipline of choosing the right compromise under constraints. Responsible lighting is no exception.

## A road that does not need a lighthouse

Picture a residential road after midnight. Traffic is sparse. The initial lighting system was designed for a busier environment and operates at the same output regardless of demand. Perhaps the lamps are spaced to illuminate the pavement uniformly under conventional standards. Perhaps a few entrances and crossings need particular attention.

The cheapest retrofit may be replacing each old fixture with a new LED at a comparable nominal light level. That can lower electricity demand while preserving the original service. A better design may take advantage of optical control, dimming, and changed operating schedules. Instead of assuming that every part of the street requires peak illumination all night, the engineer can ask where visibility is needed and under what conditions.

There are real constraints. Drivers need sufficient time to detect hazards. Pedestrians must be visible at crossings. People with visual impairments may require different illumination characteristics. Motion sensors may respond poorly to some events or produce distracting transitions. Sudden changes in brightness can be uncomfortable or unsafe. Adaptive controls create maintenance obligations and possible failure modes.

A responsible plan would evaluate these details rather than simply declare that half the light is better. It might preserve carefully designed illumination at intersections while lowering output on lightly used segments. It might shield fixtures from bedroom windows and direct light away from nearby habitat. It might use a consistent, gradual dimming schedule and a reliable way to restore higher output when needed.

The point is that LEDs make such designs feasible. Their controllability can be a benefit beyond efficiency. Yet the benefit appears only when someone asks for it in the specification.

A city that buys only the most efficient lamp has bought a component, not necessarily a good night.

## The business of perpetual visibility

Commercial lighting has its own incentives. A shop wants to be seen. A filling station wants its forecourt to appear open, clean, and secure. A sports venue wants sufficient illumination for play and spectators. A warehouse needs visibility at loading areas. A tower owner may light a façade for identity and prestige.

These are different uses with different operating schedules. The temptation is to illuminate continuously because switching and adaptation require attention, and because the cost of electricity per lumen has fallen. The environmental consequences rarely appear in the decision maker's accounts.

Lighting can also become competitive. One business adds a brighter sign. Its neighbor responds. A district increases the overall background level without making each sign proportionately easier to notice. The result resembles an arms race in attention, financed through electric bills and paid for partly through the surrounding light field.

A city can regulate aspects of signage, upward-directed lighting, operating hours, and light trespass while still allowing commerce. A business can preserve legibility with directed light, contrast, and thoughtful design rather than maximum output. A stadium can use high-performance fixtures during events and avoid projecting unnecessary light for hours afterward.

The difficulty is not that owners lack technology. It is that the benefit of limiting spill often accrues to someone else: neighbors, wildlife, astronomers, or the regional sky.

That makes lighting a governance problem. The source of the effect is private or locally managed, but its consequences are shared.

## What efficiency should mean

There is a more ambitious definition of lighting efficiency: the amount of useful task performance delivered per unit of resource and unwanted exposure.

Consider a pedestrian crossing. The useful outcome is that drivers can recognize the pedestrian in time to act safely, and pedestrians can identify the crossing and its hazards. The engineering goal is not maximum lumens or minimum watts in isolation. It is sufficient detection and comfort under actual conditions.

A fixture that directs less total light onto the right surfaces could outperform a brighter, poorly aimed one. A dimming system that adapts to lower traffic may maintain adequate visibility while reducing energy consumption and skyglow. A warm spectrum and careful shielding may reduce certain ecological exposures without compromising a task that does not require full-spectrum illumination.

Those improvements should be evaluated, not merely assumed. A full design process would measure visibility, uniformity, glare, energy use, maintenance, skyward emission, and sensitive environmental impacts. The relevant balance differs by site.

The crucial economic insight is that lower cost per unit of light should create more options. We can use less electricity for the same service, more service for the same electricity, or a combination. Those are choices. If the policy goal includes protecting the night, the choices must be made explicitly.

LEDs did not fail. They succeeded so well that they exposed the weakness in our definition of success.

## A thousand lamps and two invoices
Consider an illustrative town replacing one thousand older exterior fixtures. Suppose each old fixture consumes 150 watts and each replacement consumes 60 watts. If they all operate for ten hours per night, the arithmetic is straightforward: the old installation uses 1,500 kilowatt-hours each night; the new one uses 600. The nightly saving is 900 kilowatt-hours, before accounting for controls, maintenance, and differences in actual operating schedules.

Those numbers are hypothetical, not a report of a municipal project. They demonstrate why efficiency gains are compelling. A difference in operating cost becomes substantial when multiplied by years and thousands of fixtures.

Now imagine that the town uses the lower cost to install another five hundred lamps and leaves all fixtures operating throughout the night. Energy use may still be lower than before, depending on the actual designs, but the illuminated area has expanded. Some new light may serve genuinely unmet needs. Some may illuminate places no one required to be bright.

The energy account and the environmental exposure account have diverged.

A full evaluation would compare useful illumination at actual tasks, total energy, fixture numbers, optical distribution, operating hours, and skyward or habitat-directed spill. A town could obtain a net benefit on all dimensions if it uses better optics and controls. It could also improve efficiency while making the sky brighter.

The example exposes why efficiency alone is an incomplete policy. A luminaire's power consumption tells us how much electricity it uses. It does not tell us whether its light is needed or where it goes.

### The retrofit that raises the ceiling

An older lamp with broad light distribution may illuminate the street, nearby façades, and part of the sky. A modern LED can be engineered to put more of its output onto the pavement. That should create an opportunity to reduce total light while preserving visibility.

But a procurement specification may demand the same measured brightness as the old system in one location without checking the distribution elsewhere. The new fixture may provide an intense bright patch and sharp transitions. Residents may perceive it as harsher even if its total electrical demand is lower.

A municipality that evaluates only wattage will miss that problem. A municipality that checks only nominal lumens will miss it too. The design must be assessed from relevant viewing positions under actual conditions.

Suppose the goal is to improve visibility of pedestrians at a crossing. The important quantity may involve contrast on a person viewed by an approaching driver, not just horizontal illuminance at the pavement. A lamp could be less intense overall yet more useful if it makes the pedestrian legible.

The best retrofit may therefore specify a performance outcome and permit engineers to find the lowest-waste distribution that achieves it. The resulting fixture may save energy and reduce glare simultaneously.

This is an example of technology enabling environmental improvement rather than causing it automatically. The engineering capability creates the option. The purchasing decision determines whether it is used.

### The color-temperature shortcut

A city under pressure to reduce light pollution may adopt a simple rule requiring warm-colored LEDs. Such a policy can address a legitimate concern about short-wavelength emission. Yet the color label is only one part of the actual exposure.

Two fixtures with the same correlated color temperature can differ in spectral distribution and optical control. A warm lamp aimed into a bedroom remains intrusive. A warm floodlight left on all night near a nesting beach can remain disruptive. A poorly shielded source can produce skyglow regardless of its pleasant appearance.

Color policy becomes stronger when paired with aiming, timing, and output limits. It should also respect applications in which color rendering and detailed visibility matter. A hospital loading area may have different visual requirements from a decorative façade.

A useful procurement document would therefore specify the purpose of the installation, acceptable visual performance, permitted spill, control behavior, and relevant spectral characteristics. It would not assume that one number in kelvins resolves every environmental question.

The problem is not that simple rules are always bad. Simple rules can be easier to enforce and maintain. The problem is when the chosen simplification hides the most important physical mechanism.

### The control that stops saving energy

A motion-responsive lighting system can reduce unnecessary operation. A timer can dim or switch off fixtures during periods of low use. These controls can produce savings beyond the efficiency of the source itself, as DOE guidance notes.

But the system has to work. Sensors can fail to detect users. Controls can be overridden after complaints. Maintenance staff may leave fixtures on permanently if troubleshooting becomes difficult. A beautifully modeled reduction in annual operating hours can disappear in practice.

A realistic evaluation therefore needs a maintenance and failure plan. What state does the light enter if the sensor is unavailable? How are faults discovered? Can emergency access restore sufficient illumination? Will a rapid switch from dark to bright create glare or disturb nearby wildlife? Do residents understand the schedule?

A simpler fixed dimming plan may sometimes be more reliable than complex adaptive controls. In another site with variable activity, advanced sensing may justify its cost. The choice should follow the task and the operator's ability to maintain the system.

A lighting project should be judged several years after installation as well as on commissioning day. Durable performance is an engineering result, not a rendering.

### Counting the unused photons

If light is purchased to illuminate a particular surface, the light that escapes beyond the needed area can be viewed as a form of waste. Not every reflected photon is avoidable, and some uniformity is necessary for comfort and safety. But substantial upward, horizontal, or intrusive output often adds little to the intended service.

This is where the cheap-light paradox can be turned into an opportunity. Modern optics and controls allow more precise distribution. The owner can keep the useful light and reduce some of the wasted part. Energy savings and environmental benefits may reinforce one another rather than compete.

The public argument should focus on those interventions before it becomes a demand to eliminate nighttime activity. An efficient lamp is not the enemy. A lamp whose output is unexamined is the design problem.

The difference between a responsible retrofit and a brighter city is not merely the efficiency of the semiconductor. It is whether someone asked what the light was for and measured the consequences of delivering it.

## The invoice with missing lines

The electric bill lists consumption. A lamp specification lists lumens, watts, color, expected life, and perhaps optical distribution. Neither usually lists how much light reaches the neighbor's bedroom, how a local insect community responds, or how much contrast is lost in the sky.

Those omissions do not make the bill dishonest. They reveal how the product has historically been valued. The purchaser pays for useful illumination; the environment receives whatever the installation emits beyond that purpose.

As evidence accumulates, the omissions become less defensible. We can measure light direction, spectrum, duration, and night-sky brightness. We can test biological responses in specific circumstances. We can compare lighting designs that preserve human function while reducing spill.

The mature approach is not to assign an invented dollar price to every moth or star. It is to impose reasonable performance requirements on outputs that no buyer has a right to waste without limit. Many environmental standards work this way. They do not require every effect to be priced perfectly before a design improvement becomes justified.

The cheap-light paradox is a sign that our technology outran our habits. The LED made better lighting possible. Whether the world becomes darker, brighter, safer, or more ecologically disrupted depends on how we use it.

A more efficient second sun can still erase the night if we choose to make it larger. The answer is not to condemn efficiency. It is to put purpose back in charge of illumination.
