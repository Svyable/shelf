# 4 — The Map That Hid the Stars

The planet is easiest to admire at night when it is photographed from above.

In familiar orbital images, coasts are stitched with light. River valleys become strings of settlements. Roads reveal themselves as connections between bright concentrations. Borders sometimes appear because the economic activity, electrification, and urbanization on either side differ. The images can make human civilization look like a living network.

They also teach a misleading lesson: that the brightest regions are the places where the most important things are happening.

A dark area is not necessarily empty. It may be forest, pasture, desert, ocean, protected land, a community with limited grid access, or a place where lights are directed down rather than toward a satellite. A bright area is not necessarily prosperous or safe. It may contain energy-intensive industry, gas flares, a stadium, or a city whose lamps emit substantial light without delivering proportionate benefit.

Nighttime satellite imagery is a scientific resource with constraints, not a moral map. The instrument sees what its detector is able to receive. The things that matter to a human standing on the ground can be different.

That mismatch became particularly interesting when people began reporting that their stars were disappearing faster than satellites seemed to indicate.

In January 2023, Christopher C. M. Kyba, Yiğit Öner Altıntaş, Constance E. Walker, and Mark Newhouse published a [study in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abq7781) using observations from the Globe at Night citizen-science project. The team analyzed more than 51,000 visual observations collected from 2011 through 2022. Rather than directly measure electrical consumption or upward light emission, volunteers compared the stars they could see with charts depicting different sky-brightness conditions.

The researchers estimated that the declining visibility was consistent with an average annual sky-brightness increase of 9.6 percent over the sampled locations. That rate implies a doubling of sky brightness in roughly eight years if sustained, but the estimate is an average from volunteer observations, not a measured trajectory for every city or a forecast guaranteed to continue.

The discrepancy with some satellite trends was not a scandal. It was a reminder that a map produced by one instrument does not necessarily represent what another observer experiences.

## What a satellite cannot see

A satellite detector measures radiation within a defined range of wavelengths, angles, spatial resolutions, and times. No image contains every possible kind of light.

The Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite, or VIIRS, includes a day-night band that has been widely used to map nighttime illumination. The instrument is valuable for detecting changes in settlement lighting, outages, and large-scale emission patterns. Yet its spectral response does not reproduce the human eye. Certain short-wavelength output from modern lighting is poorly represented, and the satellite's view of upward emission differs from a person experiencing light scattered across the horizon.

The Kyba paper specifically drew attention to spectral shifts and emission geometry as plausible explanations for the difference between satellite observations and citizen reports. Light emitted horizontally can brighten the sky seen by nearby people without contributing in the same way to the upward radiance recorded at a particular satellite viewing angle. Shorter-wavelength emissions can also affect perceived sky brightness differently from what a particular instrument registers.

The consequence is subtle. A city could replace older lamps with fixtures that emit less light in one satellite-sensitive band, while residents experience a sky that appears no darker—or even brighter. A policymaker might celebrate the satellite trend without noticing the local human view.

That does not mean satellites are unreliable. It means they are reliable for the quantities they measure within their known limitations. Combining instruments is how scientists learn more.

A useful analogy is the difference between a thermometer and a humidity sensor. Both report something about the same air, but neither can replace the other. If an observer feels uncomfortable despite a stable temperature, it would be foolish to declare that discomfort impossible because the thermometer did not change. One would examine humidity, wind, radiant heat, and other conditions.

For light pollution, the corresponding measurements include upward radiance, ground-based sky brightness, spectral composition, glare, and biological exposure. Different questions require different instruments.

The challenge is political because maps feel complete. A colorful global image invites confidence. Its boundaries are not always visible, and its missing variables can be mistaken for missing consequences.

## Citizen science as an instrument

The Globe at Night method turns thousands of people into a distributed observational system. Participants do not need a satellite or observatory. They need the ability to identify a constellation, compare the stars visible from their location with reference charts, and record the observation under conditions suitable for the program.

This is a clever use of human vision. The observation incorporates part of the visual sensitivity that matters to ordinary people. It can reveal changes in perceived sky brightness that a detector with a different spectral response might understate.

It also introduces errors and biases that a scientific paper must account for. Volunteers are not placed randomly around the planet. They are more likely to participate where astronomy education, internet access, and awareness of the program are available. Weather can obstruct stars. Atmospheric transparency changes. Participants may vary in vision, skill, and attention. The locations of observations can change over time.

The Kyba team used a large dataset and statistical analysis to infer a trend, but the method cannot be described as a perfectly balanced census of the world's nighttime sky. The reported global average is a model-based estimate from participating places. A city absent from the data cannot be assigned that rate simply because it appears on the same map.

The paper's conclusion is nevertheless important: in the locations and periods sampled, the decline in visible stars was consistent with rapidly increasing sky brightness. The result fits broader evidence that human nighttime illumination is extensive and changing.

The interesting methodological question is how to combine the two kinds of evidence. A satellite may show where radiance is emitted; a ground observer reports what the sky looks like; a local fixture audit identifies what can be changed. Together they provide more actionable information than either could supply alone.

A city that wants to know whether its new streetlights reduced light pollution should measure more than electricity demand. It could record the color and aiming of fixtures, measure ground-level sky brightness under comparable conditions, check lighting uniformity and glare at relevant pedestrian locations, and examine local ecological exposure where appropriate.

The evaluation need not be impossibly elaborate. Even a modest set of repeated, well-documented observations can distinguish a real improvement from an assumption that LED efficiency automatically means a darker sky.

## The atlas with a warning label

The 2016 world atlas by Falchi and colleagues was a landmark because it made the scale of light pollution visible in a more systematic way than individual night photographs could. It integrated satellite information, propagation modeling, population data, and ground-based brightness measurements. The resulting estimates described how much artificial skyglow many people were living under.

The commonly repeated headline—approximately 83 percent of humanity under light-polluted skies—is striking. It is also easy to misuse. The threshold for the atlas's light-polluted category was defined in relation to natural zenith sky brightness. The percentage is not the share of people who can see no stars. Nor does it mean 83 percent of Earth is lit at the same intensity. It is a population-weighted estimate under a specified brightness definition.

The atlas also reported that more than a third of humanity could not see the Milky Way from their skies under the modeled conditions. Even this is not a statement about every moment. Weather, season, moon, local obstructions, and eyesight matter. The claim concerns typical sky-brightness conditions and the loss of visibility of a diffuse feature.

A methodological warning is useful here: the more memorable a statistic becomes, the more its original definition tends to disappear. A number turns into a slogan. The slogan travels farther than the paper, and eventually someone cites it as though it were a direct measurement of everything the number might imply.

That is not a reason to avoid numbers. It is a reason to make their boundaries part of the argument.

For example, the atlas described artificial brightness at the zenith, the point directly overhead. A resident's horizon can be significantly brighter than the zenith, especially near a city. An observatory may be affected by a light dome on one side of the sky even when its overhead view is relatively dark. A turtle hatchling may respond to a bright coastal lamp that hardly changes regional zenith sky brightness.

The environmental footprint of light is spatially complicated. One figure cannot capture every relevant direction.

## The politics of a dark pixel

A night-light map is often used to study electricity access, economic activity, urban growth, or disaster impacts. Those applications can be useful when limitations are understood. A dark region may suggest inadequate electrification, but it can also indicate sparse settlement or outdoor-lighting practices that produce less upward emission.

The distinction matters because darkness carries contradictory political meanings. In one setting, it can signal the absence of a basic service. In another, it is a protected environmental quality. In a third, it may coexist with reliable electricity and a deliberate choice not to illuminate an entire landscape.

A global argument for reducing artificial light cannot ignore communities that still lack reliable power and safe public infrastructure. It would be morally absurd to tell a village that its nights ought to remain dark for the sake of satellite images while residents want electricity for a clinic, a school, or secure movement.

The correct target is not human development. It is wasteful and harmful light output relative to the function being served.

This requires better questions than whether a region is bright or dark. Can people use the street safely? Does a hospital have reliable electricity? Are lamps aimed at the intended task? Is a nearby habitat exposed to unnecessary light? Can fixtures be dimmed when no longer needed? Does the environment have meaningful glare or skyglow?

Darkness in a satellite image may be evidence of scarcity, good design, or simply low population density. A planner should not infer one from the pixel alone.

The same care is needed when evaluating cities. A wealthy neighborhood may have elaborate decorative lighting that spills into a less wealthy neighbor's windows. An industrial corridor may illuminate nearby homes without providing useful pedestrian safety. A poorly lit street may genuinely need better lighting. The policy should be able to distinguish all three.

The goal is not to rank communities by darkness. It is to manage light as infrastructure whose benefits and burdens are distributed unevenly.

## What a map leaves out

Astronomical maps often emphasize the cumulative glow from many sources. For ecological research, the relevant exposure may be far more local and intermittent. A single floodlight near a bat roost can affect emergence behavior. An illuminated bridge can change how insects gather over water. A beachside fixture can misdirect hatchlings even if the coastline is dark at the scale of a satellite pixel.

Conversely, a city can have considerable skyglow without every individual lamp being equally problematic. The fixtures causing the greatest upward emission or strongest habitat exposure may be concentrated in particular places. Targeting those sources may produce disproportionate benefits.

This is why an inventory matters. A city needs to know who owns the lights, where they are, what their optical characteristics are, and when they operate. A streetlight system might be mapped in municipal records while private outdoor fixtures remain poorly accounted for. Commercial and residential lighting can make substantial contributions that a public utility's retrofit cannot fully control.

Even accurate inventory does not reveal every effect. Vegetation can screen a lamp in summer and expose it in winter. Snow can change reflection. Clouds can reflect urban emissions. Construction can alter sightlines. A biological species may be present only during migration or breeding. Exposure changes through time.

An effective map must therefore be interpreted as a tool for selecting interventions, not a complete description of nighttime experience.

The same principle applies to the data made from citizen reports. An individual observation is informative but noisy. A long-running collection can reveal patterns, provided that the analysts account for where participants are and how conditions change. A one-time dramatic photograph of a starless sky cannot establish a long-term trend. A controlled series can.

Scientific instruments extend our senses by making certain variables measurable. Their limitations are part of their power, because a known limitation can be corrected with another measurement.

## The child and the changing sky

Kyba and colleagues included a vivid illustration of the trend they inferred: over a childhood, a location that once showed many stars could show substantially fewer if the measured rate of sky-brightness growth persisted. The illustration communicates a cumulative environmental change that happens too gradually to feel like an event.

That image is stronger when held alongside its uncertainty. The study does not predict the future star count of every child's backyard. It describes a trend inferred from observed sky visibility across volunteer locations during 2011–2022. Policies, fixture design, development, weather, and future technology can change local outcomes.

The point is not to predict that a particular child will lose a precisely calculated number of stars. It is to recognize that a generational environmental shift can proceed without a widely noticed moment of loss.

There is another way to make the problem tangible. Ask whether people in a city can identify a place within reasonable reach where the Milky Way remains visible. If they cannot, the loss is not merely statistical. It has changed what a person can experience without substantial travel.

The answer might lead to dark-sky park programs, regional lighting ordinances, school astronomy nights, or targeted urban lighting changes. Those are different interventions with different costs. A society can decide that restoring some view of the sky is valuable even if it cannot restore a pristine sky everywhere.

The important point is that the decision be deliberate. An absence of deliberation has been the default policy for the light we emit.

## A satellite's blind spot is not a conspiracy
When a ground-based study reports a faster increase in sky brightness than a satellite-based study, the discrepancy can become a story about hidden data. That interpretation is unnecessary. Scientific instruments are built with explicit capabilities, and different instruments can measure different properties of the same environment.

The VIIRS day-night band was not designed to reproduce the precise spectral sensitivity of a dark-adapted human eye. Its detector records radiation within its own response curve. The human visual system weights wavelengths differently, and the scattering of light in the atmosphere can change the relationship between emitted radiance and observed skyglow.

A city replacing sodium lamps with white LEDs can therefore change the measured spectral composition even if the total useful illumination remains similar. Depending on the instruments and geometry, a satellite may record a decrease in part of the output while a person sees an unchanged or brighter sky.

The appropriate response is instrument comparison. Researchers should publish the spectral assumptions, calibration methods, field conditions, and limitations. A city evaluating a retrofit should not rely on one sensor that may be insensitive to a relevant component of the light.

This is how scientific disagreements become productive. One measurement reveals a pattern; another appears to disagree; the difference identifies a missing variable. The solution is not to distrust all maps. It is to learn what each map measures.

### The denominator no one notices

A percentage change in sky brightness sounds simple until the baseline is examined. A 10 percent increase in one already bright urban sky represents a different absolute exposure from the same percentage increase in a relatively dark location. The biological or astronomical consequences may depend on the absolute level, not only the relative rate.

The 9.6 percent annual estimate reported by Kyba and colleagues is also an inferred trend over the locations represented in the Globe at Night dataset. It should not be converted into a claim that every location brightened by exactly that rate. Some locations may have improved, others worsened, and some remained relatively stable.

A local policymaker needs both the regional pattern and the specific baseline. If a city's sky is already highly brightened, reducing a major source may improve visibility without restoring the Milky Way. In a darker town, preventing a new upward-directed installation may preserve a valuable existing condition.

The policy objective can differ. Restoring dark skies is not the same as slowing further deterioration. Protecting the remaining dark regions is not the same as reducing glare for people in an urban neighborhood.

A statistical average is useful for describing a trend. It is a poor substitute for identifying the intervention that will help a particular place.

### How a volunteer can help an instrument

A citizen-science observation can be informative without being perfectly precise. A volunteer reporting visible stars is using a sensory system whose spectral response is relevant to human experience. The observation can complement a physical detector, particularly when the study involves repeated measurements across many places and times.

But the volunteer also brings variability. Eyes differ. People may misidentify constellations. Atmospheric conditions can change. Artificial glare from a nearby source may affect the observer even when the overall sky background has changed little. Participation may be concentrated in regions with active astronomy communities.

A credible analysis does not pretend those biases vanish because the dataset is large. It tests for patterns that might arise from sampling and incorporates uncertainty. The result can still be valuable, especially when combined with other evidence.

There is a practical opportunity for schools and communities here. Students can learn to record the same sky from the same location under comparable conditions, noting cloud cover, moon phase, time, and nearby lighting. Repeated observations can teach the limits of individual perception while building a meaningful local record.

The exercise is not a substitute for professional monitoring. It is a way to make the night a measurable part of the environment rather than an assumed background.

A child who learns to count stars may become more interested in the sky. A city that learns to measure changes in sky brightness may become better at managing its light.

### A map that can guide action

A regional sky-brightness map becomes most useful when connected to actual fixtures and decisions. Suppose a municipality identifies a bright light dome along one edge of its territory. The dome might arise from a commercial district, industrial installation, or neighboring town. The map alone does not identify the responsible source.

A more actionable investigation would combine ground observations, local inventories, operating schedules, and perhaps temporary lighting adjustments where cooperation is possible. If a particular site dims its fixtures during a controlled period, repeated measurements may help estimate its contribution to the surrounding sky.

Such a test needs care. Weather and atmospheric transparency can vary between nights. A large city may have many overlapping sources, and one small change may be difficult to detect. The absence of a visible change in a regional map does not prove the intervention had no local benefit.

The town should therefore define success at several scales. Did the target site's upward emission decline? Did nearby residents experience less glare? Did the wider sky respond measurably under comparable conditions? These are distinct results.

The strongest policy may come from combining direct physical design changes with longer-term monitoring, rather than expecting one dramatic image to prove everything.

### Reading the dark pixel with respect

The ethical difficulty of nighttime maps is that low radiance can represent different realities. A protected natural area may be dark because its ecological condition is being deliberately preserved. A remote community may be dark because it lacks reliable electricity. An area may appear dark because its lighting is directed in ways a satellite does not record well.

A responsible analyst should not flatten these situations into a moral ranking of brightness. Electricity access can support health, education, safety, and economic opportunity. Dark-sky conservation should not be used to argue that communities ought to remain without basic infrastructure.

The appropriate goal is more efficient and less harmful illumination where illumination is needed. A village receiving electricity can install well-directed lights. A wealthy city can reduce unnecessary spill without surrendering essential services. The same technical principles can support both.

A satellite image can reveal broad human patterns but cannot tell us whether a place has enough useful light, too much unwanted light, or both. That judgment requires additional evidence and the people who live there.

The map should widen the questions we ask, not replace them with one color scale.

## Measuring a recovery

Suppose a town announces that it will reduce skyglow. What would count as success?

An electricity bill is not enough. A lower bill may result from efficient lamps that still produce unwanted illumination. A satellite image is useful but may miss changes in spectrum and horizontal emission. A photograph can inspire but is vulnerable to exposure settings, weather, and selective framing. The town needs a baseline and a method.

A practical measurement plan would document fixture type, luminous output, shielding, operating hours, and spectral characteristics before changes. It would measure sky brightness under repeatable conditions. It would assess glare and roadway visibility at the places where people actually use the light. If sensitive habitats are involved, it would include relevant biological monitoring rather than assuming darker skies automatically imply ecological success.

The project should also identify tradeoffs in advance. A reduction that saves energy but makes a dangerous crossing difficult to see may require redesign. An intervention that improves star visibility but leaves excessive light entering bedrooms may be incomplete. A turtle nesting zone may need seasonal restrictions even if annual sky-brightness averages barely change.

Success is therefore multidimensional, but it is not immeasurable. Different instruments can answer different parts of the question.

The strongest public policy would publish the measurements and the uncertainty instead of announcing an unqualified victory. If skyglow fails to improve, investigate whether private sources, regional light domes, clouds, or spectral shifts explain the result. If visibility improves and safety remains acceptable, the town has evidence that more precise lighting works.

This is the lesson hidden in the disagreement between satellite maps and people counting stars: the night does not belong to one sensor. We can illuminate a place usefully while reducing what we waste into the rest of the sky. But we will not know whether we have succeeded until we measure what the relevant observers actually see.

The map is a beginning, not the night itself.
