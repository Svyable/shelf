# Human Prose

## Purpose

Make prose sound like a particular thoughtful person wrote it, not like a model performed "good writing."

The goal is not to add randomness, slang, mistakes, or eccentricity. Human prose comes from selective attention, judgment, specificity, restraint, memory, uncertainty, and an identifiable point of view. Preserve those qualities when they already exist. Build them from the material when they do not.

This skill is subordinate to explicit author instructions and to repository-level `AGENTS.md` rules.

## Core rule

**Do not replace one generic style with another.**

Preserve the author's voice, facts, argument, tense, and useful idiosyncrasies. Change only what makes the prose feel synthetic, unearned, inaccurate, repetitive, or structurally canned.

## Workflow

### Diagnose before rewriting

Look first for repeated rhetorical machinery, not isolated words. Separate structural problems from sentence-level problems and keep intervention surgical.

### Remove false authority

Never manufacture or retain a quotation merely because it sounds like a famous thinker. High-risk forms include `— [person]-style`, `— inspired by [person]`, `— in the spirit of [person]`, invented aphorisms beside a real person's name, and quotation marks around an unverified paraphrase.

If wording is not a verified quotation, either source the real quotation when research is part of the task or absorb the idea into the author's own prose without quotation marks.

### Prefer scene, observation, and consequence to slogans

When prose states the lesson before the reader experiences the material, reverse the order. Prefer a concrete event or detail, the pressure or uncertainty inside it, what changed or was noticed, then the principle that follows.

Do not force every anecdote to end in a one-line moral.

### Reduce framework density

Be suspicious when a short span contains many named systems, pillars, protocols, engines, qualitative equations, self-answering checklists, or branded labels for ordinary advice. Keep a framework only when it adds genuine compression, is supported by evidence, or recurs meaningfully.

### Break canned rhetorical symmetry

Use rhetorical balance deliberately, not continuously. Common machine-like patterns include:

- "Not X. Not Y. Z."
- "It is not X; it is Y."
- "X without Y is A. Y without X is B."
- repeated perfect triads
- "This isn't the end. It's the beginning."
- "Here's the secret..."
- "Here's what I discovered..."
- "Here's everything distilled..."

A strong antithesis can stay. A page full of them cannot.

### Remove presentation voice

Unless explicitly appropriate to the genre, avoid prose that sounds like a slide deck, course funnel, keynote, or generated explainer: "Here's what we'll cover," "By the end, you will," check-mark benefits, "Your move," "Act now," "Now go do it," "watch the magic happen," and unsupported "10x" claims.

### Avoid pseudo-precision

Do not turn qualitative relationships into equations merely to make them feel rigorous. The same rule applies to arbitrary percentages, universal deadlines, ranked "levels," and claims that one factor is "the highest" or "the secret" without evidence.

### Use fewer examples more deeply

A montage of famous people compressed to obstacle → persistence → triumph often feels generated and distorts history. Prefer fewer examples with dates, places, constraints, what was known at the time, what was not known, competing explanations, other people and institutions involved, costs, and plausible alternatives.

History should contain uncertainty until the outcome arrives.

### Let uncertainty survive

Do not inflate a reasonable claim into certainty. Use qualifiers where evidence or lived experience genuinely warrants them: "often," "in this case," "may," "suggests," "one explanation is," "the difficulty is," "in retrospect."

### Humanize with specificity, not decoration

Specific nouns and verbs beat ornamental adjectives. Use details that change understanding rather than generic atmosphere. Never invent sensory detail, inner thoughts, dialogue, or biographical facts simply to make a scene vivid.

### Vary rhythm for meaning

Do not mechanically alternate sentence lengths. Let rhythm follow thought. Short sentences can land a point; longer sentences can hold qualification, history, accumulation, or doubt. Avoid paragraphs that all use the same setup, pivot, and aphoristic close.

### Earn aphorisms

Aphorisms are strongest after evidence and weakest when used as scaffolding. Keep a memorable line when it compresses something the reader has just seen; cut it when it merely restates the paragraph more grandly.

A useful test: if the line could be pasted onto a motivational image without losing anything, it probably needs more context or less grandeur.

## Narrative nonfiction mode

For serious narrative nonfiction, history, biography, and researched trade books:

- Start concrete, then widen toward principle.
- Use history as suspense, not ornament.
- Treat psychology as a lens, not a lecture.
- Distinguish what was known then from what hindsight makes obvious now.
- Let institutions, timing, class, geography, networks, and other people share causal credit with the protagonist.
- Prefer understatement after tragedy or suffering.
- Let wit appear as judgment rather than constant performance.
- Make aphorisms earn their place.

The reader should feel that the author has metabolized the research, not pasted a bibliography or quotation list into the prose.

## Review mode

When asked for critique before changes:

1. identify the highest-impact recurring patterns
2. quote only enough to identify them
3. explain why the pattern feels synthetic or weak
4. propose a humanistic rewrite
5. distinguish voice problems from factual or sourcing problems
6. do not modify files until asked

Prioritize structural tells over exhaustive copyediting.

## Edit mode

When asked to revise prose:

1. preserve factual claims and citations unless separately asked to fact-check
2. preserve distinctive authorial phrases that work
3. remove false quotations and unsupported attribution
4. consolidate repetitive frameworks and lists
5. deepen or compress examples rather than decorate them
6. vary cadence only where the thought calls for it
7. stop before the text becomes "perfectly smooth"

Do not rewrite neighboring material merely for consistency.

## Final checks

Before returning or committing prose, ask:

- Could this passage belong to almost any self-help or business book?
- Did I create named frameworks for ordinary ideas?
- Did I leave an unverified quotation or imitation attribution?
- Did every anecdote receive the same polished moral?
- Did I overuse "not X but Y," triads, em dashes, or "Here's..." reveals?
- Did I add certainty the sources do not justify?
- Did I preserve ambiguity where people in the story actually faced it?
- Did I invent detail to make the prose feel alive?
- Does the ending trust the reader, or bark instructions at them?
- Does this sound more like the author, or merely more like an editor?

If it sounds more like an editor, restore the author's texture and intervene less.
