# 1 — The Night Has a Ceiling

The birds arrived before the city had finished sleeping.

On the morning of October 5, 2023, people working around Chicago's McCormick Place Lakeside Center found the ground covered with small bodies. Most were migrating songbirds. The Field Museum's long-running collision-monitoring effort recorded 964 dead birds at the building. They came from 33 species, according to contemporary reporting. The extraordinary count was not a normal night on the lakefront. It was the result of an ugly combination: a pulse of migrating birds, weather that pushed them lower, illuminated glass, and a building positioned where the migration route ran along Lake Michigan.

[Audubon's October 6 account](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/more-1000-birds-collided-single-chicago-building-one-night) describes the tally and the conditions. A 2021 [Field Museum report on a peer-reviewed study](https://www.fieldmuseum.org/about/press/turning-lights-can-save-migrating-birds-crashing-buildings) had already shown that the brightness of windows was associated with collision risk at this same building: when half its windows were dark, the researchers observed many fewer bird strikes than on comparable nights when they were all illuminated.

It would be convenient to tell the story as a simple indictment of city light. It is not. Glass itself is a danger to birds because a transparent surface can appear to be an opening and a reflective surface can appear to continue the sky or trees. Weather altered the birds' flight. Migration density mattered. Window illumination mattered. The building was an intersection where all those factors met.

That is precisely why the event belongs at the beginning of this book. Our lights do not act alone. They alter an older environment in which the rest of life is already making decisions.

A bird navigating after sunset has not chosen darkness as a lifestyle. It is operating within a light regime that existed long before the skyline. Its sensory and navigational systems were shaped in relation to sunlight, twilight, moonlight, starlight, cloud, and seasonal change. We built a new regime above it, below it, and directly in its path. The transition was so useful to us that we mostly stopped noticing it.

There is another turn in the Chicago story. After the deadly 2023 migration season, McCormick Place added a patterned treatment to large areas of its glass. In autumn 2024, researchers reported a decline of more than 95 percent in bird collisions at the treated windows compared with earlier years. The reduction was reported in [McCormick Place’s January 2025 announcement](https://www.mccormickplace.com/newsroom/press-release-2025-01-08/). That improvement should not be credited to darkness alone: the important new intervention was making glass more visible to birds. The measures can complement one another.

This matters because the wrong lesson from a tragedy can lead to the wrong repair. If the account becomes "lights killed a thousand birds," a city may believe that switching off a few lamps solves the entire problem. If the account becomes "glass killed the birds," it may ignore decades of evidence that bright interiors and exterior illumination can aggravate nocturnal collision risk. The useful story is not that one villain was caught. It is that multiple adjustable parts of the environment turned an ordinary migration into a mass fatality.

The city can change some of those parts without surrendering its ability to function at night.

## A crime scene with a skyline

Chicago was not built to be an obstacle in a bird's mental map. The lakefront is a human arrangement: roads, parks, rail, convention halls, towers, piers, windows, floodlights. The lake is far older. Migrating birds use large-scale environmental cues and respond to weather and habitat. The modern shoreline confronts them with concentrated structures and artificial light.

There is a revealing difference between the view from a hotel room and the view from the air. A visitor sees the glittering lakefront as an achievement. An urban planner sees an economic waterfront, a destination, a network of paths and public spaces. A bird moving at night sees something closer to a change in the sensory physics of the landscape. Some light draws attention; some prevents a safe route through a transparent barrier from being recognized; some changes the appearance of the horizon.

These perspectives are not moral equivalents. People have legitimate needs for light. A worker leaving a late shift should be able to find an entrance, identify a hazard, and reach transportation without unnecessary danger. Emergency services cannot operate on the premise that the world ought to be black. A city cannot treat every illuminated window as proof of negligence.

But the existence of necessary light cannot excuse every unnecessary photon. We are not required to choose between a dangerous darkness and an indifferent glare. That is an engineering false choice. Brightness has a direction, a spectrum, a duration, and a purpose. It can be designed.

The McCormick Place record is unusually valuable because scientists were collecting evidence long before a dramatic headline. The Field Museum's work extended across decades. It made it possible to examine how collisions varied with lighting and weather rather than inventing a causal story after a single catastrophic night. The 2021 study of lighting effects found sharply different collision counts when windows were lit versus darkened. Those results do not imply that every building would show exactly the same factor of change, but they establish that lighting was not a poetic detail. It was part of a measurable risk.

The window-film improvement added a second layer: physical visibility can be changed too. One intervention alters the attractant or disorienting environment; the other makes the barrier legible. Neither required eliminating Chicago, prohibiting glass, or telling millions of people to go to bed at sunset.

The practical implication extends beyond birds. When artificial light creates a problem, our first instinct is often to ask whether we should have less light. The more useful question is what the light is doing. Does it illuminate the intended surface? Does it enter a bedroom window? Does it create glare that obscures a darker object? Does it scatter upward? Does its spectrum affect animals differently? Does it remain on after anyone needs it?

A lighting fixture can perform its intended function while producing a much larger footprint of unintended illumination. That footprint is part of the infrastructure, even when it does not appear on the procurement sheet.

## The sky that became private

For most of human history, the night sky was not a specialized recreational amenity. It was an ordinary part of the world. People could see stars by stepping outside under suitable conditions. They could use the sky for travel, timekeeping, farming calendars, storytelling, worship, speculation, and the enjoyment of looking up. Cloud and weather still obstructed the view. Moon phases changed its brightness. The horizon was often broken by mountains and forests. The sky was not uniformly clear, and the past was not uniformly beautiful.

Today, in many cities, the first obstacle is not cloud. It is our own light.

In 2016, Fabio Falchi and colleagues published a [world atlas of artificial night sky brightness](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1600377) in *Science Advances*. Using satellite data, light-propagation modeling, and ground measurements, they estimated that roughly 83 percent of humanity lived under light-polluted skies, including more than 99 percent of the population of the United States and Europe. They estimated that the Milky Way was no longer visible to more than a third of humanity, including nearly 80 percent of North Americans.

These were modeled population estimates for the conditions and data used in 2016, not a census taken on every clear night of 2026. The numbers should not be repeated as though the world stood still for a decade. They are nonetheless extraordinary. An experience that once required a clear night can now require an itinerary.

A person living under a bright urban sky can be born, go to school, work, raise children, and die without ever seeing the band of the Milky Way that our solar system occupies. That does not mean the person is ignorant, deprived of meaning, or less connected to nature than a rural neighbor. It means that a feature of the physical environment has been removed from ordinary access by the way cities operate.

There is an economic oddity in this loss. No one receives a monthly bill showing how many stars disappeared. The light that obscures them is bought by many different owners for many different purposes: roads, signs, stadiums, factories, homes, parking lots, towers. The cost of the skyglow appears elsewhere, spread across people who did not consent to the combined output.

A single lamp owner may have little reason to care about light escaping above the horizontal. A neighborhood full of owners creates a different sky. The problem resembles noise pollution, but light is particularly easy to mistake for a benefit, because its useful and unwanted parts are produced by the same equipment.

The good produced by a lamp is concentrated where its illumination falls. The harm can be distributed far beyond the intended target. The person who gains a brighter parking area may not be the person who loses a dark bedroom, a quieter night habitat, or a view of the sky.

That is not an argument for making darkness a luxury product, available only at resorts and national parks. It is an argument for treating an otherwise shared environment as something worth deliberate management.

## Not all night is dark

One reason the argument gets confused is that night is described as an absence. The sun has set; light is gone; civilization provides it. That picture ignores a rich natural system.

On a clear, moonless night away from artificial illumination, the sky still has luminance. Stars contribute, but so do airglow produced in Earth's atmosphere, zodiacal light created by sunlight scattered from interplanetary dust, and faint diffuse celestial backgrounds. With the moon above the horizon, illumination can change dramatically. Snow, clouds, water, leaves, and stone reflect what light is available. A night landscape does not hold one uniform value of darkness.

Animals and people have evolved or developed ways of using these differences. Human eyes can become substantially more sensitive after time in the dark. Rod photoreceptors support vision at low light levels, while cones provide color vision under brighter conditions. The transition is gradual and context dependent; a person with fresh exposure to glaring headlights will see a different night from someone who has been walking in darkness long enough to adapt.

A bright lamp can interfere with that adaptation even while improving visibility nearby. It can reveal a step directly beneath it and conceal a pedestrian outside its pool by contrast. It can make a route feel safer while leaving the next stretch more difficult to see. Good lighting design requires attention to contrast and glare, not simply total brightness.

There is a second mistake inside the phrase "natural darkness." Wild animals are not always safer, healthier, or more successful in the darkest imaginable conditions. Moonlight can increase predation risk for some species and improve foraging for others. Different animals respond to different wavelengths and light regimes. Some urban species exploit insects attracted to lamps, while others avoid illuminated corridors. The biological question is not whether light is inherently bad. It is how a particular species' behavior and survival change when a particular light source is introduced.

A sea turtle hatchling and a human bicycle commuter both make decisions from visual cues. Those decisions occur in different bodies, different tasks, and different environments. A warm amber lamp shielded away from a nesting beach may be an improvement over a bright unshielded white lamp, but even it can be problematic under certain conditions. A pedestrian crossing a busy road may need illumination that enables drivers to see them clearly. The right outcome cannot be derived from a blanket preference for more or less light.

That is the central claim of this book: artificial light is an intervention in an environment with many simultaneous users. We designed most of it around the user most able to buy the switch.

## The machine that reaches beyond its bulb

The light that leaves a fixture does not stop where the fixture's owner intends. Some photons strike a road, table, wall, or face. Some enter an eye. Others scatter from surfaces and the atmosphere. Clouds can change the distribution and apparent brightness of a city at night. Light that travels upward may scatter back into the view of someone kilometers away.

This makes artificial light different from an ordinary manufactured object. A lamp sits at a location; its output changes a volume. The output can cross a property line without passing through a gate. A homeowner may never have agreed to live beside a parking-lot floodlight, yet a room in the home can be lit by it. A person may never attend a stadium, yet the stadium's lights can contribute to regional sky brightness.

The effect is difficult to value because light is often purchased under a different description. A business buys safety, visibility, advertising, image, operating hours. A city buys pedestrian confidence, road recognition, surveillance capability, or a sense that the public realm is maintained. The useful service is real. But an installation can oversupply illumination because there is no clear price on the spillover.

It can also be oversupplied through habit. The sign remains illuminated because it always has been. A parking lot is lit at the same level after closing as during peak use. A building façade glows to express prestige to an audience that may be asleep. A pathway uses the same lamp specification whether it runs beside a wildlife corridor or a retail entrance.

The development of efficient light-emitting diodes complicated the economics. A light source that consumes less power for the same output should make it easier to save energy. It can also encourage a city or property owner to install more fixtures, light previously unlit surfaces, and keep installations brighter or operating longer. Energy efficiency and environmental improvement are related, not identical.

The invisible output of an LED is not only electrical demand. The fixture produces illumination with particular directions and wavelengths. It modifies a nighttime habitat that extends beyond the road or wall it was purchased to light. In later chapters we will follow that effect through insects, birds, beaches, lakes, human clocks, and astronomical instruments.

For now, the important distinction is that a bulb's price and power rating are not an adequate account of its consequences.

## A photograph that cannot show the loss

Images of Earth at night taken from orbit have become icons of the modern world. Cities trace bright arteries along coasts and rivers. Roads reveal settlement patterns. The economic map appears without borders drawn on it. Regions under conflict or power outage can sometimes be recognized by a change in nighttime emission.

Those images are extraordinary measurements. They also encourage an odd reversal. We begin to see a bright patch as evidence that an area matters. A dark patch can appear empty, remote, poor, or undeveloped, even when it contains a functioning ecosystem, a community with different energy infrastructure, or simply a landscape whose night has not been illuminated from above.

An orbital night-light image is not a direct photograph of everything people can see on the ground. Instruments have specific spectral sensitivities, spatial resolutions, viewing geometry, and detection thresholds. The amount of light emitted upward is not the same as sky brightness at a person's eye. Some contemporary LED spectra and horizontally directed illumination are underrepresented by the measurements used for certain long-running satellite datasets.

This matters because a map can say that upward radiance is changing slowly while observers report a rapid decline in visible stars. The difference need not mean one group is lying. They may be measuring different parts of the light field.

In 2023, Christopher Kyba and colleagues analyzed 51,351 citizen-science observations submitted from 2011 through 2022 to the Globe at Night program. Their [*Science* paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abq7781) estimated that the changes in visible stars corresponded to an average annual sky-brightness increase of about 9.6 percent over the locations sampled. This is an inferred trend from a volunteer dataset, with geographical coverage and participation biases, not a uniform measured rate for every point on Earth. Its importance is that human eyes were telling a story about the changing sky that simple satellite comparisons did not fully capture.

A person standing in a city might not notice the change from year to year. If each childhood sees fewer visible stars than the last, there may be no single night when the loss feels like an event. The sky has no door that slams. It just becomes less available.

That is a peculiar way to alter a planet: incrementally, through millions of individually reasonable decisions, without anyone needing to intend the whole outcome.

## Counting what a building does
The 964 bodies collected at McCormick Place after the October 2023 migration event are a recorded count, not necessarily a complete measure of every collision. An animal may strike a building and die beyond the monitored area. It may survive initial impact but later die from injuries. A scavenger may remove a body before the survey. Searchers can miss small birds among plants, structures, and traffic. The work of counting collisions therefore includes a detection problem.

That does not make the Chicago count uncertain in the sense that it should be dismissed. It means the measured number has a specific meaning: birds found under the monitoring procedures at that location and time. A rigorous scientific assessment distinguishes observed recoveries from estimates of total mortality. Doing so makes a future intervention easier to evaluate.

Suppose two seasons produce different recovery totals. The difference could arise because lighting changed, windows were treated, migration volume shifted, weather conditions differed, or search effort varied. Some causes may occur together. A reduction in observed bodies is encouraging, but a causal explanation requires looking at the relevant alternatives.

This is why decades-long monitoring is so valuable. It creates a record of ordinary variation against which an extraordinary night and a major design change can be interpreted. The Field Museum did not begin asking whether Chicago's glass was dangerous only after the event made national news. Its researchers had spent years collecting evidence about collisions at the convention center.

There is a larger lesson about environmental harms that become visible through patient counting. If nobody had collected the birds, the night might have become a story about unusual migration calls or a few isolated deaths. An unmeasured cost can remain politically invisible even when it is physically real.

The monitoring program was itself infrastructure, built from people, procedures, identification skills, specimens, and records. It made a new decision possible.

### The comparison nobody gets for free

A building owner may reasonably ask how much a particular intervention will help. Turning off interior lighting, treating windows, changing façade materials, and adjusting landscaping have different costs and mechanisms. There is no trustworthy shortcut to estimating their effects without considering the building and its conditions.

The 2021 lighting study at McCormick Place used the available historical variation in illumination to evaluate collision differences. The 2024 window-film results concerned a later physical alteration of glass. These are related but not identical pieces of evidence. One cannot simply add their reported percentages or assume that a combined intervention guarantees the product of two reductions.

The interventions may act on overlapping pathways. A light can attract or alter the movement of birds toward a hazardous façade. A visible window treatment can reduce the chance of collision once a bird is close. Eliminating either pathway may change the opportunity for the other to cause harm.

This is precisely why the practical recommendation is to address both when feasible, rather than to treat one study as a license to ignore the other. A bird-visible façade remains useful in daylight and on nights when some interior light is needed. Reduced illumination can help during migration even at buildings whose glass has been treated.

A good future study could compare treated and untreated sections under carefully documented lighting conditions, weather, and migration intensity. It would need to avoid interpreting differences caused by where birds naturally concentrate as effects of the treatment alone. The design would be more complex than a simple before-and-after photograph, but it would tell owners what they need to know.

The question is not whether the intervention looks compassionate. It is whether it changes the relevant hazard.

### A building as an experiment

Modern buildings are equipped with systems that can be measured and controlled. Lighting circuits, blinds, occupancy schedules, and façade treatments can be adjusted. A building near a migration route can therefore function as a carefully managed environmental experiment without being built for scientific purposes.

A responsible protocol might begin with a historical baseline: number of collision recoveries by date, weather conditions, migration estimates where available, search area, and monitoring effort. It would record the illumination state of major window surfaces and identify façade treatments. It would also document changes in nearby construction and landscaping that might alter the birds' flight paths.

The analysis should report outcomes with appropriate denominators and uncertainty, not just one dramatic total. A decline in collisions during a season of very low migration intensity is less informative than a similar decline under comparable migration pressure. Yet perfect comparability may be impossible, and the report should acknowledge that.

There is also a difference between scientific monitoring and operational management. A building owner need not wait until every causal detail is settled before switching off unnecessary light. The evidence for avoidable risk may already be sufficient. Monitoring can then evaluate whether the chosen changes work and identify additional repairs.

The most effective public response often combines precaution and learning: make a low-cost change supported by existing evidence, then measure the outcome rather than declaring success in advance.

### The building and the weather

Weather made the 2023 Chicago event particularly severe. The birds were moving along a major migration route, and conditions changed their flight and exposure to buildings. In the public retelling, weather can become a convenient excuse: if the unusual storm caused the disaster, perhaps the building owner was powerless.

The opposite mistake is to pretend weather was irrelevant. A building can be more or less dangerous depending on atmospheric conditions, and no façade treatment controls the wind or rain. Good risk management considers the intersection.

This distinction appears in other environmental hazards. A flood may require heavy rain, but the damage depends partly on construction, drainage, and land use. A fire may require an ignition source, but building materials and suppression systems shape the outcome. A collision may be triggered by migration weather, while illumination and glass determine the risk of impact.

The fact that an event has several causes does not absolve the people who control one adjustable cause. It also does not justify blaming them for every cause.

A useful safety question is how a design performs under foreseeable adverse conditions. An urban building near a migration corridor should be assessed not only on a typical clear night but during periods when many birds move at lower altitude. The building may not be able to prevent every collision, but it can reduce its contribution to the hazard.

This is the difference between waiting for a perfect night and designing for the nights that actually happen.

### The body count and the missing stars

The birds supplied the first visible cost in this book because the event was tangible. The invisible costs of skyglow are harder to place on a table. A missing star does not leave a specimen. A disrupted insect visitation pattern may take repeated observations to establish. A person's sleep problem may have several causes.

That difference in visibility affects which problems become urgent. The dramatic loss can produce action quickly, while chronic environmental change may proceed with little notice. A strong research program has to make both types of harm measurable.

This is why the case does not end with mourning. It ends with new measurements and changed glass. Once the physical pathway was understood, the design could be improved. The intervention was not perfect proof that lighting alone caused every collision, nor did it need to be.

A city has many such opportunities. Some will produce measurable biological benefits. Others will reduce glare, energy use, or unwanted nighttime exposure. The evidence will be strongest when the intended outcome is identified before the changes occur.

Chicago's bird count is more than a warning. It is an example of what becomes possible when a cost hidden in the built environment is counted over time, and when that count is allowed to change the design.

## What the birds made visible

The Chicago collision event forced people to confront something the skyline had concealed. It made the costs of an illuminated environment countable, at least for one building on one night. The subsequent window treatment demonstrated that the arrangement could be changed. A tragedy became evidence for specific design decisions rather than a reason for despair.

Yet the night remains a more complicated public resource than a single collision count can measure. There are workers who depend on seeing their way home. There are people who fear dark public spaces for sound reasons. There are children who need to sleep near roads. There are bats using illuminated insects, moths whose feeding behavior changes under lamps, turtles following the wrong horizon, scientists whose telescopes record scattered city light, and cities whose identity includes the glow of their streets.

No one gets to erase those competing interests with a slogan.

The best outcome is not a country in which every light is turned off. It is a society that knows what its light is for, aims it where it is needed, measures what escapes, and changes the operating schedule when the original purpose ends.

We began with birds arriving at a lakefront building in the wrong conditions. The building did not move; the migration was ancient; the light field and the glass were human choices. Once those choices were examined, some could be changed without giving up the useful city around them.

We often speak of darkness as something we defeated. It may be more accurate to say that we made darkness harder to find, without realizing how many living systems were still using it.

The night has a ceiling now. We built much of it ourselves.
