# WE STOLE THE NIGHT — Research trail

**Evidence cutoff:** November 16, 2026.

**Form:** Investigative narrative nonfiction with primary-source-linked evidence, historical interpretation, field ecology, human biology, city-design research, and explicit policy tradeoffs.

## Central question

What becomes of a planet when artificial illumination is cheap enough to supply indiscriminately, and how can cities recover useful darkness without sacrificing the genuine human benefits of lighting?

The strongest competing answer to the book’s thesis is that illumination is largely a net benefit and that improvements in efficiency, safety and urban access outweigh local environmental costs. The manuscript does not rule that answer out by definition. It distinguishes cases where additional lighting reduces harm from those where extra output creates exposure without useful service.

## Evidence hierarchy

1. Peer-reviewed original field experiments, randomized interventions, and measurements, with the observation and causal-inference limits stated.
2. Peer-reviewed reviews and meta-analyses as syntheses of heterogeneous evidence.
3. Official technical, conservation, and historical institutions for operational or documentary context.
4. Expert historical interpretation, contemporary reporting and museum records for specific events, not as substitutes for biological experiments.
5. Hypothetical examples explicitly presented as design scenarios, not completed field research.

## Source chronology

- **1807 / 1882:** Pall Mall gaslight demonstrations and Pearl Street central electric station are historic events, described through museum and government history sources. Earlier public oil-lighting systems also existed.
- **2016:** Falchi global atlas modeled population exposure to skyglow; its headline percentage is not a 2026 direct census.
- **2017:** Knop field experiment documented reduced nocturnal flower visits and focal plant fruit set under defined Swiss meadow lighting conditions.
- **2019–2020:** IARC evaluated occupational night shift work and published Monograph 124; this does not classify every streetlight or screen as a human carcinogen.
- **2019:** Chalfin et al. randomized NYC public-housing lighting trial reported reduced nighttime outdoor index crime; specific intervention and setting.
- **2015:** Steinbach et al. England/Wales interrupted time-series study covered 62 authorities and found little evidence of aggregate harm from certain lighting adaptations; observational limits remain.
- **2023:** Kyba et al. inferred rapid change in perceived star visibility from 2011–2022 volunteer observations.
- **October 2023:** McCormick Place mass bird collision recorded after a severe migration/weather event; glass and lighting were distinct factors.
- **August 2024 / January 2025:** Bird-visible window-film installation at McCormick Place and subsequent >95% decline reported in a site-owner release based on fall migration monitoring.
- **2026:** Peer-reviewed bird meta-analysis, field radar experiment, timed insect-light comparison, lake vertical-distribution study, and field bat context study support different, context-specific responses.

Do not treat study date, publication issue date, field-observation year, and a media recap as interchangeable. Corrected author metadata for the Diaz-Palma paper was noted in July 2026.

## Claims that are deliberately *not* made

A reduction in recorded bird strikes after window film cannot be wholly attributed to darkening lights. The 9.6% annual sky-brightness trend is an inference from volunteer sites, not a constant for each address. The 62% change in nocturnal flower visits is not a global crop-loss fraction. IARC's Group 2A label is for night shift work, not every nighttime photon. A randomized result on crime in NYC does not mean that doubling brightness everywhere cuts crime by 36%; a British null association does not imply every unlit road is safe. Some species benefit from lamp-attracted insects while others avoid the same location. A warmer appearance alone is not proof of ecological suitability.
