# CHORD

*A Testable Theory of Time, Access, and the Continuity of Thought*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

An image can remain in the visual system without being the image a person reports seeing. A neural population can change its coding geometry in less than twenty milliseconds. Recurrent processing can take longer to bind features into a coherent relation. A machine can remember an action and still fail to distinguish its effects from what happened elsewhere.

These observations do not establish a universal clock of consciousness. They do give us an uncomfortable set of questions about how information becomes available, how it survives interruption, and how a system knows when to revise its account of the world.

CHORD follows a research program that tried to turn those questions into software experiments. Its results are less tidy than a manifesto. Some gates improved estimation under scarce bandwidth, then failed when noise increased. Reliability-aware sampling repaired part of that failure. A learned representational switch showed a small numerical advantage, but disabling the switch did not harm the model in the way the hypothesis predicted.

The book treats those results as a reason to refine the mechanism, not declare a breakthrough.

The science includes peer-reviewed primate and human studies, a prominent 2026 preprint on alternating perceived and suppressed representations, clinical perturbational research, theories of global access, and the more ordinary disciplines of state estimation, causal inference, and software systems. They are not interchangeable forms of evidence. Where an experiment measures behavior or neural activity, the claim stops there. Where the book proposes an architecture, it is a proposal. Where the subject turns to subjective experience, uncertainty remains.

The synthetic TACH laboratory results are unpublished engineering experiments conducted in constructed environments. Their recorded values, model names, and stated limitations should not be read as independent demonstrations in biological neuroscience. The source trail identifies relevant primary publications, publication dates, and the experiment provenance used here.

The reader does not need to believe a machine is conscious to care about this project. A system that can select useful information, preserve causal beliefs, recover from interruptions, and distinguish its own actions from external events would already be an important engineering achievement. Whether such functions are sufficient for experience is a further scientific and philosophical question.
