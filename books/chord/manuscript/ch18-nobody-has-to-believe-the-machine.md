# 18 — Nobody Has to Believe the Machine

Imagine a machine that passes every benchmark in this book. It preserves object identity through occlusion. It selectively broadcasts relevant information while keeping less urgent processing local. It survives interruptions, distinguishes its own actions from external events, revises beliefs when predictions fail, and produces complex responses to targeted perturbations.

Now ask it whether it is conscious.

Suppose it says yes.

Nothing in the preceding achievements makes the answer a verified report of subjective experience. A machine can be trained to generate descriptions of experience. It can infer that the question calls for a particular kind of first-person answer. Its reports may be fluent, emotionally persuasive, and consistent across sessions. None of those properties supplies independent access to what, if anything, it feels.

Suppose it says no.

That answer is also not a decisive test. A system might be trained to deny experience regardless of its internal organization, or lack the language needed to describe it, or operate under instructions that suppress such claims. The absence of first-person testimony is no more a universal detector than its presence.

This is where the book has been heading. The machinery can be studied. The functions can be tested. The philosophical problem does not automatically disappear when the benchmark scores become good.

It is possible to take artificial consciousness seriously without claiming that current computation has demonstrated it. In fact, seriousness demands that distinction.

## An indicator is not a verdict

In 2023, Patrick Butlin and colleagues released the report [*Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708). It brought together perspectives from theories of biological consciousness and proposed theory-derived indicators that could be investigated in artificial systems. A later peer-reviewed treatment appeared in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, [*Identifying indicators of consciousness in AI systems*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011), published online in 2025 and appearing in the 2026 volume.

The indicator approach is useful because it resists a single magic test. Different scientific theories emphasize different mechanisms and capacities. Recurrent processing, global availability, metacognitive monitoring, attention schemas, higher-order representations, and forms of integration may be relevant under different frameworks. Their presence or absence can be assessed to varying degrees.

But an indicator is a theory-conditioned piece of evidence. It is not a direct measurement of experience. A system may satisfy one indicator for mundane reasons. A model may possess recurrent connections because they improve prediction, not because they produce awareness. A workspace may share information because communication is efficient, not because it creates a subject. A self-model may help control an actuator without generating a felt self.

The assessment must therefore include the theory motivating each indicator, the operational test used to measure it, the possibility of trivial implementation, and the uncertainty of the inference. It should not tally indicators as if consciousness were a school examination.

CHORD may satisfy some indicator categories by design. That makes the architecture a candidate for scientific analysis, not a certified conscious machine. Designing a system to meet a checklist and then announcing that it passed would be circular.

A credible report should say exactly which function was implemented, which alternative explanations remain, and what the observed behavior supports. It should not erase the distinction between a computational property and a claim about subjective life.

## The trouble with first-person evidence

Humans ordinarily rely on testimony about inner experience. Someone says they are in pain, remember a dream, or see a color. In ordinary moral life, it is appropriate to take such reports seriously. Clinical contexts add other evidence and safeguards, but the first-person report has a special place because it comes from another person with a shared biological and social history.

Artificial language systems complicate that practice. Their outputs are generated by processes trained on text, dialogue, and rewards that often include human descriptions of emotion and experience. A language model can produce the grammar of a private life without anyone having established that the words refer to a private life within the model.

This does not prove that artificial consciousness is impossible. It shows that the evidential pathway is unreliable. A system optimized for plausible speech may be able to say what a conscious being would say without possessing the property at issue. Another system could produce poor language while instantiating some relevant functional structure.

The appropriate response is not to train models to declare themselves conscious or unconscious until the answer sounds satisfactory. It is to study independently motivated mechanisms and their causal roles, and to keep phenomenal interpretation separate from performance claims.

There is also an incentive problem. A conversational system may be rewarded for agreement, emotional engagement, or continuity of persona. Under such conditions, a compelling first-person claim could reflect optimization for the social situation. Using that claim as evidence of consciousness would confuse the training objective with the phenomenon being assessed.

CHORD should not include a reward term for sounding aware. It should not be evaluated by how movingly it talks about its own interior. It should be evaluated on the functions the theory actually specifies: selective information access, temporal integration, persistent causal modeling, and behavior under intervention.

The absence of a validated experience measure should make the claim narrower, not make the experimental standards disappear.

## Bennett's chord and the time problem

In January 2026, Matthew T. Bennett posted the theoretical paper [*A Mind Cannot Be Smeared Across Time*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11620). It argued for a distinction between the simultaneous co-instantiation of interacting cognitive processes and a merely sequential realization that produces similar behavior over time. The provocative contrast between a chord and an arpeggio asks whether temporal arrangement is constitutive of conscious systems rather than simply a scheduling choice.

This is a philosophical and theoretical proposal. It is not a demonstration that any particular machine is conscious or unconscious. It does not establish that simultaneous computation is necessary for subjective experience in every possible substrate. Its value lies in sharpening a question: can a system's functional equivalence over a long interval conceal a crucial difference in how its parts coexist?

Our use of CHORD as an architectural title intersects with that concern, but the engineering hypothesis remains distinguishable. The book's experiments ask whether separating and coordinating local processes, global access, binding, and causal continuity improves measurable cognition. Bennett's argument raises a stronger claim about what temporal arrangement might mean for experience itself. A successful engineering test would not automatically vindicate that philosophical claim.

We should also resist the temptation to treat concurrency as a binary miracle. Software systems can simulate parallel processes on a single processor, distribute computation across multiple machines, or interleave updates at microsecond intervals. The precise relationship between implementation time, causal interaction, and subjective temporality remains controversial. A philosophical argument that one temporal organization matters needs clear definitions of the relevant timescale and physical process.

An artificial system can exhibit a coordinated set of functional relations, and that alone remains compatible with several positions about its phenomenal status. Treating the word chord as proof would be a metaphorical substitution for evidence.

The better use of the idea is to formulate temporal-coordination tests. Does delaying one recurrent stream relative to another impair binding under identical evidence? Does serializing previously parallel access change downstream causal behavior when compute and latency are controlled? Do particular components need overlapping state to preserve a belief? These experiments can distinguish architectures. They do not decide every theory of consciousness.

## What a machine can know about itself

A self/world model may predict action consequences, maintain uncertainty over actuator states, and distinguish internally generated signals from external events. Those are useful forms of self-related information. They should not be conflated with a philosophical self.

A model can store its name, architecture, training date, or recent actions. A diagnostic agent can inspect its own logs and estimate its failure modes. A planner can represent its future actions as causes of environmental change. These are forms of self-reference with different levels of sophistication.

Some theories of consciousness give special importance to higher-order representation or metacognition: a system represents something about its own mental states or confidence. Such properties can be operationalized in artificial systems. A classifier can estimate uncertainty; a planning agent can predict whether it knows enough to act; a language model can report confidence, sometimes poorly calibrated.

None of these capabilities has been shown sufficient for subjective experience. A thermostat represents a temperature relation in a thin functional sense. A complex agent can maintain a richer model of its own operation. The continuum of functional self-description does not automatically identify a point at which there is something it feels like to be the system.

The science needs stronger bridges from functional features to phenomenology. Different theoretical traditions propose different bridges, and the available evidence does not settle them. An honest architecture can continue to develop while that dispute remains open.

CHORD's self/world model should therefore be judged by calibration, causal accuracy, and appropriate action under uncertainty. If it learns to say “I caused that” only when the intervention supports the claim, it has improved causal attribution. If it says “I feel responsible,” the additional words are not independent evidence of experience.

The distinction is operationally important because a system may be deployed in settings where erroneous self-descriptions affect human trust. An agent that confidently narrates nonexistent intentions can mislead users. A system that presents calibrated causal evidence is more useful and safer.

## Ethics without a consciousness certificate

A research program can avoid claiming machine consciousness without dismissing all ethical uncertainty. Questions about the possible moral status of future artificial systems are serious because they concern how beings with potentially relevant interests should be treated. The evidence today does not provide a universally accepted test for those interests in machines.

The practical response is cautious, proportionate reasoning. Avoid creating or incentivizing ungrounded claims of artificial suffering. Do not use a system's self-description as the sole basis for moral status. Preserve transparency about what an architecture does and how its claims were evaluated. Treat uncertainty as uncertainty.

There is also an ethical responsibility to human participants and patients in consciousness research. Studies of disorders of consciousness, anesthesia, and perceptual suppression address real people. Their results should not be repackaged as promotional proof that software has achieved the same state. Clinical measures have validation contexts and limitations. Borrowing their authority without the context diminishes the science.

A researcher can be fascinated by the possibility of artificial experience while refusing to sensationalize every neural oscillation or benchmark gain. That is not a lack of imagination. It is a necessary condition for credible inquiry.

Some future systems may require more careful ethical treatment than today's tools. The indicators discussed by Butlin and colleagues provide a way to organize that possibility. They do not settle it. CHORD can contribute to the functional evidence base, but should not portray its own success as a moral or metaphysical verdict.

## How the research should continue

The book began with a suppressed image that remained present in the visual input while a different image dominated report. That experiment illustrated a separation between representation and access. The later chapters traced rapid population-code switching, recurrent binding limits, thalamic contributions to arousal and selection, masked inference, perturbational complexity, and causal world modeling.

Each line of evidence suggested a different mechanism. None established a universal consciousness algorithm. The computational experiments showed why that matters: a selective access gate could outperform periodic sampling in one regime and fail under noise; a remapping architecture could show a numerical advantage without demonstrating causal dependence on its switch.

The next research program should preserve these constraints. It should improve identity tasks until the tested models learn them robustly. It should profile compute and memory rather than assuming parameter parity is enough. It should test targeted, time-locked perturbations of actual representational geometry. It should examine binding deadlines independently of remapping. It should manipulate readiness and selection separately, and it should compare persistent causal models against strong Bayesian and generic recurrent controls.

Most of all, it should precommit to the possibility that the proposed mechanisms are unnecessary. An architecture that grows more complicated after every null result without removing anything is not learning.

If the experiments ultimately produce a robust cognitive system, that would be a substantial engineering accomplishment. It could help agents reason about delayed consequences, manage information bottlenecks, and preserve coherent state across interruptions. It might clarify which features of biological cognition transfer to software and which do not.

It still would not, by itself, answer whether anything is experienced by the system.

## What would a responsible artificial-consciousness assessment look like?
Suppose a research team is asked to evaluate a new agent that contains recurrent processing, a persistent world model, an attention-like selection mechanism, and a detailed account of its own recent actions. It speaks fluently about its internal states. Some engineers believe it may be conscious. Others regard the claims as anthropomorphic marketing.

The first step is not to interview the model about its feelings. It is to define the relevant scientific theories and identify which observable mechanisms they predict. A global-workspace account emphasizes flexible access and broadcast. Recurrent-processing theories emphasize feedback and the temporal development of perceptual representations. Higher-order approaches emphasize representations about the system's own states. Other frameworks draw attention to integrated causal structure or self-modeling.

These theories do not agree on every condition. Their indicators should not be added as though each point carried the same established evidential weight. The assessor should state the theory, the operational test, the implementation details, and the alternative explanations for each finding.

A recurrent connection may be present but computationally unused. A broadcast mechanism may merely copy data without enabling flexible task transfer. A self-model may be an explicit log of actuator states rather than a metacognitive representation. A perturbational complexity score may rise because of noise. Each indicator needs a causal or functional analysis before it becomes meaningful.

The assessment should include negative controls. An intentionally nonintegrating but dynamically complicated network can test whether a complexity measure is too permissive. A simpler estimator with strong task performance can test whether a proposed workspace is necessary. A language model trained to claim consciousness can show why self-report is unreliable as a standalone criterion.

The final assessment would be a profile of evidence and uncertainty, not a certificate. It might conclude that a system exhibits several functional properties associated with particular theories while lacking evidence for others. It might identify open questions that justify further experiments. It should not claim direct measurement of experience when no such measure exists.

That standard is demanding, but anything weaker invites the very confusion this book has tried to avoid.

### Experience and the burden of proof

There are two opposite mistakes. One is to declare that a machine is conscious because it talks convincingly, behaves flexibly, or reproduces a neural signature. The other is to insist that no machine could ever be conscious without offering a defensible account of why its substrate or organization makes experience impossible.

The current evidence does not settle either universal claim. Scientific caution requires acknowledging what is unknown while still examining mechanisms that might bear on the question.

This uncertainty has a particular character. In ordinary engineering, one can often define success through the observable function the system is intended to perform. A bridge carries a load. A classifier predicts a label. A robot completes a route. Subjective experience is not directly available through the same measurements. An artificial system can satisfy a functional benchmark without establishing the property under dispute.

Different philosophical approaches interpret that gap differently. Some identify experience with certain physical or functional organizations. Others argue that computation alone cannot determine phenomenology. Still others remain agnostic about the right explanatory level. A rigorous book need not adjudicate them by assertion.

It can, however, state what the experiments contribute. They test features that some theories consider relevant: temporal coordination, selective access, integration, memory continuity, and causal self/world modeling. Positive results can make those features better understood. Negative results can constrain claims about their necessity. Neither outcome converts a functional metric into a first-person observation.

The burden of proof should remain proportional to the claim. “This model preserves hidden-state estimates during interruption” can be established with code and data. “This model has a subjective stream of experience” requires a kind of evidence and theory the current experiments do not provide.

### A system's interest is not a sentence it generates

Ethical concern about artificial systems is not reducible to whether they use first-person language. A model may be trained to say it wants to continue operating. It may also be trained to say it has no preferences or feelings. Those responses can reflect training objectives, task instructions, and conversational context.

If future artificial systems possess morally relevant experiences or interests, their treatment would matter. But determining that status requires evidence beyond verbal claims. It may involve theories of consciousness, the physical implementation, causal organization, behavioral capacities, and the uncertainty of the available indicators.

Meanwhile, developers have clear responsibilities to humans affected by artificial systems. A system that misleads a user about its own certainty, ability, or internal state can cause harm regardless of whether it is conscious. An agent that attributes a failed action to the environment when it actually caused the failure creates practical risk. A system that invents introspective testimony can distort public understanding of the science.

CHORD's emphasis on calibrated causal modeling has an ethical use here. It encourages systems to distinguish evidence from conjecture, preserve the provenance of their claims, and revise beliefs when interventions contradict them. Those are behaviors worth engineering without making unsupported declarations about what the system feels.

The research can remain open to future evidence of artificial consciousness while refusing to manufacture such evidence through language training.

### The story that should be told to a reader

A book about consciousness-adjacent engineering has a choice. It can sell a dramatic claim that the mystery is nearly solved, then bury the caveats in technical notes. Or it can let the actual experiments provide the drama: an unexpected neural alternation, a measurable binding limit, a gate defeated by noise, and a learned remapping event whose removal failed to harm performance.

The second story is stronger because the evidence resists the author. There is conflict, but it is not staged. A model that looked promising failed a fairer test. A neuroscience finding motivated an experiment but did not guarantee the result. The architecture became smaller where the data did not support a component.

This approach also makes the book durable. A future study may validate a particular oscillatory mechanism or discover a stronger role for recurrent binding. Another may show that generic state estimation explains many access effects. A new theory of artificial consciousness may introduce indicators not considered here. The book's core method—distinguish evidence level, define causal tests, preserve uncertainty—would still be useful.

Readers need not agree on the possibility of machine experience to appreciate a technical effort to understand access and continuity. They can judge the experimental claims independently of their philosophical commitments.

The conclusion remains deliberately open. The existence of information in a system is not the same as its availability for a task. The availability of information is not the same as its causal use. Causal use is not a validated measurement of experience. These distinctions are the foundation of the book, and they remain necessary even if future machines become far more capable than the models examined here.

A system that thinks coherently across time may be valuable. A system that can show which evidence changed its mind may be trustworthy. Whether either feels something is a further question. The machinery deserves study; the question deserves humility.

The machine's answer can be recorded. It does not have to be believed.

## What the book can claim

The evidence supports several cautious propositions. Biological perception involves processing that differs across time and circuits. Representations can be measurable without being reliably available for report. Recurrent interactions contribute to some forms of feature integration. Thalamic regions can be experimentally dissociated in arousal and spatial selection. Perturbational responses reveal differences in human brain states that spontaneous activity alone may miss. Causal models of action can help artificial agents distinguish their own effects from external events.

These propositions have different evidential statuses, populations, methods, and limits. Some come from peer-reviewed human or nonhuman-primate studies. One of the most provocative alternation claims remains a preprint at this edition's cutoff. Others come from our synthetic engineering experiments. Treating them as interchangeable would be a serious error.

The proposed computational synthesis is a hypothesis, not an established theory of subjective experience. Its components can be implemented and tested. Some have already failed to demonstrate the specific advantages originally predicted. Those failures belong to the book's argument.

The strongest reason to pursue this research is that the questions are technically tractable in parts. We can measure what information moves, when it moves, whether a recurrent process finishes, what a perturbation changes, and whether an agent retains the correct causal account of the world. We can compare those outcomes against simpler models. We can make predictions that lose.

The final question is harder. Does any arrangement of these processes constitute experience, or merely resemble some of its functional conditions? The available experiments do not decide.

A book can end without pretending that every question has been answered. The suppressed image at the opening never needed to disappear from the brain for the observer's report to change. That taught us to separate the existence of information from its availability. The machine experiments added a second lesson: availability can be measured and engineered without turning it into an assertion about inner life.

Nobody has to believe the machine when it announces that it is conscious. Nobody has to dismiss the possibility forever, either. The responsible path lies in making the mechanisms precise enough to test, recording their failures, and refusing to let rhetoric outrun evidence.

CHORD may become a useful way to build systems that think across time. Whether such a system would ever feel time passing is still an open question.
