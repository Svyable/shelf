# 12 — Night Workers Without Wages

A bat can lose dinner without losing its habitat on a map.

A roost remains in the same cave or building. The insects are still present in the surrounding landscape. The route to a feeding area may remain physically open. Yet a new light near the exit can delay the animal's emergence until the most useful period of prey activity has passed.

A study of wild least horseshoe bats examined this problem through an experiment with white LED illumination near the roost. The researchers reported that exposure delayed average emergence by about fourteen minutes and sharply reduced the proportion leaving during the illuminated interval. The effect occurred even when a separate perceived-predation treatment was considered. The [primary research indexed in PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34265558/) describes how the delay could create a mismatch between the bats' departure and an early-evening peak in available insects.

The finding illustrates a kind of environmental loss that is not obvious from satellite maps or population counts. The roost is not demolished. The food is not necessarily eliminated. The animal is delayed.

For a person, fourteen minutes can seem trivial. In the life of a small nocturnal mammal whose foraging opportunity may be concentrated around a particular period after sunset, timing can matter.

The correct interpretation is conditional, not universal. The study involved a particular species, light treatment, and ecological setting. It does not establish that all bats lose fourteen minutes under any white lamp. In fact, other bat species can exploit the insects that congregate around lights.

That variation is one reason artificial-light ecology is more interesting than a simple story of good darkness and bad illumination.

## A feeding hour is part of a habitat

A landscape is often classified by its physical features: woodland, pasture, water, buildings, corridors. Ecologists also need to know when animals use those features. A place may be useful for feeding at dusk and unsuitable later. A predator may hunt under particular moon conditions. An insect may be active during a brief temporal window shaped by temperature and light.

Lighting changes that temporal arrangement. A newly illuminated path may remain physically passable while becoming behaviorally unattractive to a light-sensitive bat. A roost exit can become a barrier without a gate or wall.

This is an important expansion of the idea of habitat loss. A species may be excluded from usable hours or routes rather than from territory in the ordinary geographic sense. A map that records the roost and surrounding trees may show no change, while the animal's actual access to food has been altered.

The least horseshoe bat experiment is especially useful because the researchers measured emergence and linked it with prey activity. The issue was not merely whether the animals disliked light. It was that the timing of emergence could determine whether feeding opportunities overlapped with the availability of insects.

A future lighting project near roosts should therefore ask about the animal's activity window, not only the annual average brightness. Dimming for a short period after sunset might have a different ecological value from dimming at 3 a.m. The best timing would depend on local species and evidence.

Human lighting needs also vary by time. A park path may be busy early in the evening but nearly empty later. In some places, wildlife sensitivity peaks just when people are also active. That creates a genuine tradeoff rather than an easy win. A good design might use shielding, alternative routing, low-output task lighting, or protection of a dark corridor.

The goal is to preserve a function for both users without assuming their schedules are identical.

## Bats do not agree about lamps

Bats are often discussed as though they form one behavioral category. They do not. Species differ in flight morphology, habitat use, prey, predation risk, and sensitivity to light. Some forage around illuminated areas because insects gather there. Others avoid bright spaces, perhaps because of exposure to predators or disruption of familiar routes.

A [2024 study of synanthropic pipistrelle bats and prey](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2024.173699) reported that artificial light could support extended nighttime foraging by light-tolerant bats, with differences across spectral conditions. This is not evidence that light pollution benefits all bats. It demonstrates that a species exploiting a new resource can become more active while other species avoid the same environment.

The ecological consequences may include a change in competition. A light-tolerant bat may gain access to concentrated insects, while a light-sensitive species loses an effective route or feeding interval. Counting total bat passes around a lamp could suggest abundant activity without revealing which species disappeared from the area.

A 2026 study of bat responses across urban parks and naturally dark woodlands in Italy and Montenegro compared reactions to novel lighting exposure. The [reported findings](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42096857/) suggested that some light-sensitive species responded more strongly in dark woodland environments than in chronically illuminated urban parks. The pattern is compatible with context dependence and possible attenuation of response under long exposure, but one should not automatically label it beneficial adaptation or evolutionary rescue.

Animals that remain in cities may be a selected subset of those able to tolerate urban conditions. Behavior can change through habituation, selection, or ecological context. The mechanism matters, and different studies are needed to separate the explanations.

The central lesson is that a lamp can reorganize an animal community without causing every species to move in the same direction.

## The trap of counting winners

Suppose an illuminated park contains more bat activity around a particular lamp than an adjacent dark path. A superficial study might conclude that the light has improved habitat for bats. But the activity could be concentrated among a few species that exploit insects drawn to the lamp.

The result could coexist with a decline in use by light-sensitive species. It could also change insect populations and the opportunities available in nearby unlit areas. The local concentration of animals is not the same as an overall ecological benefit.

This is a recurring methodological trap in environmental science. Activity near an intervention is measured, but movement from elsewhere is not. A species may relocate rather than increase in abundance. A predator may consume more prey at a lamp while prey availability declines in surrounding habitat. The short-term behavior may be easy to observe while long-term reproductive outcomes remain unknown.

A stronger study records species-specific responses, nearby control areas, seasonal variation, and, when feasible, consequences for survival or reproduction. It should ask whether the lamp changed total habitat use or merely redistributed animals.

For many sites, such comprehensive monitoring will be expensive. That does not mean practical management must wait for perfect knowledge. Where a sensitive roost or commuting route is known, low-spill lighting can be a prudent design choice. The strength of the claim should match the evidence: reduce exposure to a known disturbance, not promise that one fixture will restore an entire ecosystem.

The same caution applies to birds, insects, and plants. Ecological responses can be mixtures of winners, losers, and species too poorly studied to classify. An honest policy needs to admit that complexity without becoming paralyzed by it.

## The border between two forests

Imagine a wooded corridor split by a road. Before lighting is installed, a bat can cross under natural night conditions. After installation, the road remains the same width, but a line of bright lamps creates an illuminated zone. A light-sensitive animal may now avoid crossing or choose a longer route.

The habitat map has barely changed. The functional connection may have changed substantially.

This is why corridors matter in lighting policy. A line of trees, stream, hedgerow, or wooded path may allow animals to move between feeding and roosting areas. Floodlighting a small section can alter the usability of the entire connection for species sensitive to light.

A lighting design can preserve a dark corridor without making the whole road dark. Shielded fixtures, spatial gaps, lower output, and careful placement may reduce the illuminated barrier. The details depend on traffic and pedestrian safety, local species, and geometry.

There is an analogy with sound barriers and fences, but the light barrier is less visible to us because we can walk through it effortlessly. A person may experience the lamps as improved safety. A bat may experience them as an exposed zone.

Neither experience should be dismissed. The engineering task is to provide adequate human visibility while minimizing unnecessary ecological fragmentation.

A site plan that labels the corridor green is not enough. Its nighttime light field is part of the corridor's functional condition.

## Time makes different winners

Artificial illumination can extend the active period of some organisms. The 2026 *Ecology Letters* meta-analysis of avian responses reported changes in daily activity, sleep-related variables, and other physiological measures across multiple studies. Yet it did not find an overall effect on the life-history traits analyzed. The results were heterogeneous, with differences among populations and contexts.

That outcome should resist simple storytelling. More nighttime activity is not automatically more success. An animal may forage longer while experiencing higher energetic costs. It may begin activity earlier because light permits it, because its internal timing changes, or because other environmental factors are present. Some changes may be plastic responses with limited fitness consequences; others may impose costs not captured by the available studies.

The same ambiguity appears in bats. A pipistrelle exploiting insects around a lamp may gain feeding opportunities, while another species loses access to a preferred dusk window. The local ecosystem becomes reorganized rather than uniformly damaged in one easily measured way.

Ecologists need to know which functions matter for long-term populations. But policy can still aim to reduce unnecessary disruption. A lamp left on after its human function ends is an exposure without an obvious benefit. Managing that exposure is reasonable even when the full biological consequences are not known.

The question of fairness also becomes sharper. The species that benefit most from human illumination are not necessarily representative of the broader nocturnal community. Designing solely around those visible winners can make other users disappear.

## The light map that bats would draw

A human lighting map might show fixture locations and expected illumination on pavement. A bat's functional map would look different. It would include corridors, roost entrances, prey-rich areas, open exposures, predation risks, and the timing of activity.

A lamp that seems peripheral on a municipal diagram could sit at a crucial route between habitats. A bright shopping area might provide insects for one species and form a barrier for another. A new roadlight might matter most during a narrow period after sunset rather than during the middle of the night.

This mismatch suggests a useful design process: identify sensitive ecological functions before deciding where illumination should be directed. A new development near a known bat roost may need a different approach from one on a highly developed street far from such habitat.

Environmental review should also distinguish direct and indirect light. A fixture need not be within the habitat to affect it. Glare and reflected illumination can travel from adjacent buildings. Seasonal vegetation can alter screening. A nominally shielded lamp can still send useful or unwanted light through different directions depending on mounting and site conditions.

The goal is not to give every bat an individually optimized streetlight. It is to recognize that a lighting plan has ecological consequences and that some can be reduced without defeating the human purpose.

That is a practical rather than romantic form of coexistence.

## The roost at the end of a lit street
A bat roost can be mapped as a point. Its usefulness depends on a network.

An animal leaving a roost needs access to feeding areas, and it may use familiar edges, trees, streams, or structures to move through the landscape. The route can involve a short journey at a particular time, when insects are abundant and predation risk is manageable.

A lamp installed at the exit changes the conditions of that journey even if it does not remove a tree or block a physical opening. A light-sensitive animal may wait, emerge in a different direction, or avoid the route entirely. The change can be subtle enough that a daytime property survey would miss it.

In the least horseshoe bat study, the delay at the illuminated roost was measured in minutes rather than hours. That is the right scale for the question. The early evening can carry a concentrated opportunity for feeding. If a bat leaves after the peak, the loss may not be recovered simply by flying longer.

The study does not establish an identical cost for every roost or species. It shows that a specific environmental alteration can change the timing of an important behavior. The next question is whether that behavior affects energy intake, reproductive success, or survival under the relevant circumstances.

An ecological assessment should therefore include activity timing as well as physical habitat. A roost present on a map may be functionally compromised during the hours it is most useful.

### An illuminated feeding trap

Some bat species exploit insects attracted to lamps. Their activity may increase around the source. This can seem to contradict studies showing avoidance of illumination. The contradiction dissolves when the animals are identified.

Species differ in flight style, foraging strategy, preferred habitat, and tolerance of exposure. A fast-flying bat feeding in relatively open air may exploit insects gathered around a lamp. A smaller or more light-sensitive species may avoid the same area because it is exposed, unfamiliar, or separated from its normal movement corridor.

The result is a redistribution of opportunity. One species can gain a concentrated feeding location while another loses a route. The fact that the first species becomes more visible to researchers does not make it representative of the entire community.

A meaningful study should therefore identify bat activity by species or functional group where possible. Acoustic recordings can help, though call identification has limitations and not every species is equally easy to distinguish. Thermal or infrared observation can add behavioral detail. Repeated surveys should include both the illuminated site and surrounding dark areas.

If total bat passes increase beside a lamp, investigators should ask whether animals were drawn from elsewhere. If one species dominates the increase, the effect on community diversity may differ from the headline count.

The same logic applies to insects. A lamp can gather prey into a visible concentration without increasing the total insect population. An apparent feeding bonanza may redistribute a resource rather than create it.

### A corridor with a closing hour

Suppose a road crosses a wooded route between two bat habitats. The road needs lighting for some nighttime users. The surrounding habitat may be used most intensively near dusk. A lighting plan could consider shielding, reduced output, or spatial gaps to preserve some dark passage.

The challenge is that the important biological period may overlap with the busiest human use of the road. It would be dishonest to promise that simply switching off the lights after midnight solves every wildlife problem. Some sensitive activity occurs much earlier.

A better design may depend on where the light is directed rather than whether it is entirely off. A lamp can illuminate a crossing while reducing sideways exposure toward a corridor. Planting, screen placement, and fixture position may contribute, though vegetation can change with seasons and is not a substitute for appropriate optics.

A site-specific assessment should identify the movement route and the visual task for people. If a dark corridor can be preserved without sacrificing necessary road visibility, the project has a tangible opportunity. If there is a conflict, the tradeoff must be documented rather than hidden.

The policy also needs to survive maintenance. A later replacement fixture can inadvertently flood the corridor that the original design protected. A conservation measure is durable only if future crews know what it was intended to do.

The corridor has a biological operating schedule. The city has another. Good management is the effort to design where their schedules meet.

### The difference between learning and selecting

A bat found in an illuminated park may appear less disturbed by new light than a bat from a dark woodland. One possible explanation is habituation: repeated exposure may alter behavior. Another is that the urban population contains individuals or species already more tolerant of illumination. Environmental conditions and prey availability may also differ.

The mechanisms cannot be distinguished by simply observing that urban animals react less. A population-level pattern can arise from learning within individuals, selection across generations, movement of tolerant animals into the habitat, or a combination.

This distinction matters because the phrase wildlife adapts to cities is often used to dismiss environmental impact. Adaptation can involve costs and exclusions. The animals that remain visible may be the ones able to tolerate conditions that caused others to disappear.

A stronger research design could follow marked individuals through changing exposures, compare related populations, or use controlled field treatments. Such studies would help separate behavior shaped by experience from the composition of the population.

Even if a species does habituate to light, it does not follow that every ecological function is restored. A tolerant animal may alter its feeding schedule or remain exposed to different predation risks. The observed absence of avoidance is only one outcome.

The prudent design principle is to reduce unnecessary exposure rather than assume that wildlife will eventually learn to tolerate whatever we install.

### An environmental survey with an empty evening column

A development application may include detailed daytime surveys of vegetation and animal habitat. The maps can be accurate and still miss much of the ecological use occurring after sunset.

If survey effort stops in late afternoon, a roost exit, commuting corridor, nocturnal flower network, or feeding pattern may not be observed. The resulting impact assessment can describe the physical habitat without measuring when it is used.

Artificial-light evaluation requires a time dimension. What is active at dusk, midnight, and pre-dawn? Which species are present seasonally? Which existing sources shape their behavior? What new fixture would alter the exposure?

The answer need not be a costly multi-year study for every small project. Screening can identify likely sensitive locations—known roosts, protected habitats, wetlands, dark corridors—and direct more intensive assessment where it is warranted. Ordinary projects can follow baseline rules for shielding and limiting unnecessary output.

This is how environmental assessment becomes proportionate. It does not pretend that all developments are identical, and it does not demand perfect ecological knowledge before allowing any light.

The important change is to acknowledge that a nighttime environment has inhabitants even when the surveyor has gone home.

### A species that benefits does not erase the one that loses

The strongest reason to retain both light-avoiding and light-tolerant bats in the book is that their differing outcomes prevent a false universal conclusion. Artificial light can create a new resource concentration for one species and a barrier for another. The community may become reorganized in ways that a simple total count obscures.

A responsible lighting policy should recognize that tradeoff. It can preserve necessary human illumination while avoiding gratuitous exposure in known sensitive areas. It can use monitoring to determine whether a design reduced the expected disruption.

There is no requirement to imagine every nocturnal animal as fragile or every lamp as a threat. The evidence is more complex and more interesting. The physical world can be shared more intelligently when we understand which species use which parts of the night.

The roost may remain exactly where it was. What changes is whether the animal can leave at the hour that matters.

## A night with more than one economy

We often value nighttime landscapes through the activities people can perform in them. A restaurant stays open, a road carries freight, a factory operates, a park becomes available for evening exercise. These are measurable benefits.

Other forms of work continue after dark without wages: pollination, predation, seed dispersal, decomposition, reproduction, and movement between habitats. The processes do not show up in a local lighting budget, though they can support ecological functions on which human communities depend.

The fact that the organisms are not paid does not make their work valueless. Nor does it mean every disturbance can be converted into a precise monetary loss. The appropriate response is to include their environmental requirements in design where evidence supports doing so.

The bat studies show that the same illumination can create feeding opportunities for one species and reduce them for another. That complexity should prevent a universal rule that all lights are harmful. It should also prevent the opposite conclusion that insects under lamps mean the ecosystem is thriving.

The most responsible question is whether the installed light is necessary for its human task and whether its unwanted reach can be reduced.

There is room for people to use the night without occupying every part of it.
