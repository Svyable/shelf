# 8 — The White Window

A bird does not encounter glass as a material with a building code.

It encounters what its visual system can perceive. A transparent wall may appear to be open space. A reflective façade may contain an apparent continuation of trees or sky. At night, an illuminated interior can become part of a confused visual scene, especially when weather forces migrating birds lower or when bright sources change their movement.

These are different mechanisms, and that difference matters. A bird can hit a window in daylight without any artificial light drawing it there. A nighttime light can attract or disorient a bird without a collision occurring. The deadly intersection involves movement, habitat, weather, structure, and visual cues.

Chicago's McCormick Place supplied unusually strong evidence because researchers collected birds over decades. The Field Museum's 2021 account of a study in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* reported that nights with half the windows darkened had far fewer collisions than nights when all the windows were lit, after accounting for other factors. During spring migration the reported difference was roughly elevenfold; during fall migration, roughly sixfold. These are estimates from a particular building and historical dataset, not universal conversion factors for every city.

The [Field Museum's description](https://www.fieldmuseum.org/about/press/turning-lights-can-save-migrating-birds-crashing-buildings) emphasizes that light is one factor researchers can manage. The 2023 mass collision and subsequent window treatment added a terrible and then encouraging public demonstration. The result was not simply that lights could be switched off. Glass itself could be made less deceptive.

The story is more useful if we refuse to make it simpler than the birds' actual problem.

## A migration older than the skyline

Many birds migrate at night. The timing can offer ecological and physiological advantages, including different atmospheric conditions and opportunities to feed during daylight, though the reasons vary among species. Navigation involves an array of cues that can include celestial information, geomagnetic signals, landscape features, and learned or inherited orientation mechanisms.

The public phrase birds navigate by the stars is partly true for some species, but it should not be turned into a complete description of avian migration. Different species and circumstances involve different cues. A bird does not carry one universal star chart and ignore every other signal.

Artificial illumination can interact with this navigation in several ways. Bright areas may attract birds, change flight paths, or alter altitude. Clouds and low visibility can intensify risks. A lit façade can create a visual target near a transparent surface. The actual collision depends on what the bird does next.

A controlled study can reveal one part of that system by varying lighting, while long-running monitoring can reveal how weather and migration volume interact with building characteristics. The most convincing evidence comes from both kinds of approach.

The Chicago dataset is valuable because the researchers did not begin collecting evidence after the most newsworthy event. A sustained monitoring program can distinguish ordinary seasonal variation from the effect of a management change. It also makes possible an estimate of how much harm occurs on nights that never become a headline.

A single mass collision is memorable. Many smaller nights can add up to an important total.

This matters for public policy. Building owners may assume that a rare disaster is the only reason to change. The long-term data suggest that cumulative risk deserves attention even when no single morning produces hundreds of bodies.

## The lights-out question

A lights-out program asks building owners to reduce unnecessary illumination during migration periods, particularly at times and places where risk is high. The concept is practical because it does not require a permanent ban on illumination. Offices may be empty late at night. Decorative lights may serve no safety purpose after closing. Some interior spaces can be darkened or fitted with blinds without disrupting essential operations.

The question is what happens when those changes are made.

Studies at buildings like McCormick Place provide evidence that illumination can influence collision risk. But a lighting intervention should not be used to erase the independent importance of glass. Birds can collide with untreated windows even when interior lights are off. Reflections and transparency remain.

The window treatment installed at McCormick Place after the 2023 event used patterned film that makes glass more visible to birds. The [January 2025 McCormick Place account](https://www.mccormickplace.com/newsroom/press-release-2025-01-08/) reported a major reduction in collisions at treated surfaces. The observed decline is encouraging, but a careful interpretation considers year-to-year migration changes and the different areas being compared.

The most promising approach combines measures: reducing unnecessary nighttime light, treating hazardous glass, considering landscaping and structure, and monitoring outcomes.

A building can be safer for birds without becoming unsafe for people. Stairwells, emergency lighting, and occupied work areas need not be darkened indiscriminately. The highest-value adjustments may involve decorative lighting, empty interior floors, and exterior beams that contribute little to actual nighttime activity.

This is an important example of environmental problem-solving. A building owner does not need to accept all or nothing. The available interventions target identifiable sources of risk.

## Birds are not one response

Not every bird species reacts to artificial light in the same way. Some may alter activity timing; others may avoid illuminated habitat or use it for foraging. Migratory and resident birds face different circumstances, and the effects on reproduction and survival are not identical to the effects on immediate behavior.

A [2026 meta-analysis in *Ecology Letters*](https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.70382) by Diaz-Palma and colleagues synthesized 675 effect sizes from 36 studies covering 30 bird species. The authors found consistent changes in physiology and behavior under artificial light at night, including sleep-related and activity-related effects. They did not find a consistent overall effect on the life-history outcomes they examined. Subgroup analyses suggested stronger responses in some populations, including migratory birds.

The distinction between physiological change and fitness consequence is crucial. An animal may change its timing without suffering a measurable reduction in reproduction in a particular study. It may also be affected in a way that a short study cannot detect. A meta-analysis can identify broad patterns, but it cannot establish that every species is harmed equally under every lamp.

The authors' July 2026 correction addressed the ordering of author names. That editorial detail is not central to the biology but illustrates why dates and versions belong in a research ledger.

The public narrative often leaps from the fact that light alters behavior to the claim that it is causing population collapse. Some exposures and species may indeed face serious consequences. The evidence varies, and other pressures—habitat loss, climate change, pesticides, collision hazards, and predation—often interact. A responsible book should not make light pollution the only explanation for a broader ecological trend.

The strongest policy argument can be narrower: unnecessary nighttime light changes measurable behavior and physiology, and some changes create avoidable risk. Where the function of a lamp can be preserved with lower exposure, the design deserves consideration.

That argument does not require every species to respond identically.

## The experiment in the flight corridor

A 2026 field experiment in northern Colorado examined how different light spectra affected birds migrating through a relatively low-density corridor. Researchers used vertical-looking radar over 31 nights in the 2023 and 2024 fall migration seasons and compared periods with no light to treatments using white, red, amber, and blue LED floodlights.

The [study in the *Journal of Wildlife Management*](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.70279), published online September 7, 2026, reported no detected effects on migration traffic rate under the studied conditions and only weak effects on flight direction. Broad-spectrum white light was associated with an estimated reduction in mean flight height of about 22.6 meters compared with dark control periods; the uncertainty and context matter.

It would be irresponsible to translate that result into a universal claim that white light always makes migrating birds fly 22.6 meters lower. The experiment used particular intensities, a particular location, migration seasons, radar methods, and sample of nights. A different corridor or species composition could respond differently.

The importance lies in the method. By changing light treatments in a field setting, researchers could ask whether spectral composition altered behavior even without a detectable change in the number of birds passing through. The result complicates simple narratives in which any light necessarily attracts more birds. Sometimes a change in altitude or behavior may be more relevant than an increase in traffic.

This has implications for building risk. A lower flight altitude could matter in an environment with buildings, but the Colorado experiment did not directly measure collisions with an urban façade. Linking the two would require further evidence.

The study shows what a mature field looks like: researchers separate the components of behavior rather than using the word attraction as a complete mechanism.

## Glass without intention

Glass has become a defining material of modern architecture. It creates transparency, daylight access, views, and visual lightness. It can also present hazards to birds when those animals perceive reflected habitat or apparent passage.

The issue is not that architects intended harm. The built environment was optimized around human perception, aesthetics, cost, and thermal performance. Birds use the same physical space with different perceptual assumptions. A surface can communicate one thing to a human and another to a migrating animal.

The effectiveness of patterned treatments is an example of changing that communication. Small markers can make the barrier more visible without requiring an opaque wall. Spacing, placement, and treatment quality matter; a few decorative stickers on a large window may not make the entire surface legible to birds.

There are also architectural decisions earlier in the process. Glass composition, façade geometry, orientation, landscaping, interior lighting, and site context can influence risk. A building designed near a migration corridor or wetland should not be evaluated only as a visual object for people standing outside it.

This approach is analogous to road safety. We do not expect pedestrians, cyclists, drivers, and children to perceive the same hazards identically. We design crossings, signs, and barriers around different users. A bird-friendly building extends that idea beyond the human species.

It is also a reminder that the environmental problem is often more tractable when it is treated as design rather than guilt. People can change visible surfaces and operating practices. The physical system responds.

## A decision before the birds arrive

The most difficult part of migration risk management is timing. Birds move seasonally, but the intensity of migration varies with weather and geography. A building may pose limited risk during many nights and much greater risk during a particular event. Lights-out policies can become more effective when they reflect seasonal and local knowledge rather than relying only on a permanent uniform rule.

Yet there is danger in overcomplicated control. A system that waits for perfect prediction may fail to act on a risky night. A simple seasonal practice may be more robust than a delicate forecast-based rule. The choice depends on local conditions, monitoring quality, and the costs of changing illumination.

For many buildings, eliminating unnecessary overnight light is inexpensive and useful even without an elaborate model. A closed office does not need every interior lamp visible from outside. Where safety or operations require light, shielding and window treatments can reduce risk.

The building owner should also measure outcomes. If treated windows still cause collisions, the design may need improvement. If the number of birds recovered changes across years, consider migration volume and weather rather than attributing every change to one intervention. A sound monitoring program can refine management.

There is a broader economic lesson. Bird collisions often occur outside the owner's direct financial accounting. The birds do not send invoices. Without research and public attention, the cost can remain external to the lighting decision. The Chicago event made that cost visible in a way that a conventional utility bill never could.

It should not take a thousand birds to make the design problem legible.

## When a light becomes a cue
The mechanisms by which artificial light influences migrating birds are not all identical. Bright structures can change orientation, attract birds under some conditions, or alter flight height. A transparent window can cause collision even without nighttime attraction. A city can also alter the environment through sound, habitat, and weather interactions.

The 2026 Colorado radar experiment is valuable partly because it measured several potential responses separately. If a lighting treatment changes mean flight height without a strong detected change in migration traffic rate, the finding cannot be reduced to more birds being attracted to the light. The same number of birds may pass through a corridor at different altitudes.

That difference matters when the corridor contains buildings. A lower flight height could change exposure to structures, although the study did not directly measure collisions in a city. The step from altered flight behavior to mortality would require further evidence.

A careful research program could combine radar observations with collision monitoring, weather data, building illumination, and window characteristics. It would look for patterns that distinguish attraction, displacement, altitude changes, and impact risk.

Such work is more informative than declaring all artificial light equally dangerous. It identifies which interventions are likely to matter and when.

### The birds that survived the first strike

Collision monitoring often focuses on recovered bodies, but surviving impact is not the same as escaping harm. A bird may be stunned, injured, or unable to continue migration effectively. Some survive after care; others may later die. Estimating total consequences is difficult because not every affected animal remains visible to observers.

This is another reason to separate the count from the interpretation. The McCormick Place numbers are documented recoveries under monitoring procedures. They are not a perfect measurement of all eventual mortality or injury associated with the building.

The distinction does not weaken the case for prevention. It clarifies the limits of the dataset and identifies additional outcomes that might matter. A future monitoring program could record injured birds separately, describe treatment and release where feasible, and maintain consistent survey methods across seasons.

Public reporting should resist the temptation to inflate a measured number for emotional effect. The recorded count is serious enough. Accuracy helps conservation by making future comparisons credible.

A policy based on a real count, with its limitations clearly stated, is more durable than one based on a dramatic estimate with no transparent method.

### Window film as an information design

The patterned treatment at McCormick Place is an example of designing for a sensory user different from the building's intended human occupants. A person may value the glass for views and daylight. A bird may need the glass to communicate that it is a solid surface.

The intervention changes the visual information available to the bird. It does not necessarily remove all transparency for humans. This is a fascinating case of inclusive design extending beyond our species: the same physical material becomes more legible to multiple types of observers.

The quality of a treatment depends on its coverage, pattern, placement, and upkeep. A few isolated decals on a large wall may leave extensive apparent openings. Film can degrade or be replaced during maintenance. New construction can introduce additional untreated glass.

A building owner needs a plan for treating and inspecting the relevant surfaces, not a one-time photograph of a completed installation.

The same principle applies at the design stage. Instead of treating bird safety as an optional retrofit, architects can consider glass visibility, reflection, lighting, and surrounding habitat while the façade is being planned. A preventive decision may be easier than modifying a large finished building.

Good design communicates the physical world honestly to the organisms that move through it.

### The seasonal forecast and the permanent fix

Migration occurs in pulses. Weather and geography determine when many birds move through a particular region. This creates an opportunity to increase precautions during high-risk periods, such as reducing unnecessary lights on nights when large movements are expected.

But a seasonal warning system cannot replace permanent improvements to hazardous structures. Glass remains present throughout the year. Not every dangerous event will be forecast correctly. Building management may fail to respond in time.

A robust strategy combines durable changes with operational precautions. Bird-visible glass reduces one hazard whenever birds approach. Sensible nighttime lighting reduces unnecessary attraction and illumination, including during migration. Seasonal forecasts can prompt additional actions when risk is elevated.

The approach is analogous to other safety systems. A building does not rely solely on a weather warning to prevent water damage; it also uses drainage and construction that can tolerate storms. Forecasts help manage residual risk after basic design is improved.

The important outcome is a lower expected rate of harm over many seasons, not simply a good response to the next newsworthy event.

### The species in the city you do not see

Migration conservation can change how residents perceive an urban landscape. The birds passing above buildings at night are often invisible to people below. The city seems occupied only by human commuters, lights, and traffic, while thousands of animals may be moving through the same air.

Monitoring makes that shared use visible. It shows that the skyline is part of a wider ecological corridor even when the pavement below appears completely urban.

This has practical implications for land-use planning. Buildings near major migration pathways can be assessed for glass risk and nighttime lighting. New developments can incorporate bird-visible materials and operational controls. Existing buildings can prioritize the worst hazards.

These are not abstract gestures of affection for nature. They are interventions based on a measurable collision mechanism.

Chicago's lesson is therefore larger than one terrible night. An urban building can participate in a continental migration system without anyone having intended that role. Once the connection is understood, it can be designed more responsibly.

The birds were using the night long before the convention center was built. The building can learn to share it.

## A night built for more than one traveler

The white window is a collision between two systems of interpretation. Human architecture uses glass to provide views and light. Birds can read the same surface as space that can be crossed. Human lighting extends activity into the night. Bird migration was already using the night for its own work.

The solution is not to decide that one user has an absolute right to the environment. It is to recognize that the physical setting serves more than one life. The building can remain useful, the city can remain illuminated where necessary, and the hazard can be reduced through specific interventions.

This is not a sentimental argument. It is an engineering argument supported by observational records, interventions, and species-specific research. When a surface repeatedly causes measurable harm, the design can be changed. When light contributes to risk, its timing and direction can be adjusted.

The Chicago birds did not teach us that civilization should be ashamed of illumination. They taught us that light changes what other creatures see, and that the consequences can be counted.

A window is not a promise of open space. It can be made to say so.
