# 1 — The Image That Never Left

The two images occupy the same visual system. Only one wins the report.

That is the uncomfortable beginning of any serious discussion of consciousness. Under binocular rivalry, a different image is presented to each eye, yet the observer usually experiences one dominant percept at a time. The unreported image has not been switched off at the retina. Its photons still reach the eye. Visual pathways still process information associated with it. What disappears, or appears to disappear, is the image as the object of experience.

It is tempting to draw a line through the brain: processing on one side is unconscious, processing on the other conscious. A great deal of modern consciousness research has been an attempt to find where that line belongs. Some candidates have been anatomical. Some have been computational. Others have been temporal. Each can explain an interesting part of the evidence, and each becomes less convincing when promoted into a total answer.

In October 2026, a group of researchers led by Janis K. Hesse placed a particularly awkward observation into this argument. They reported that representations of perceived and suppressed visual stimuli could rapidly alternate in recordings from primate inferotemporal and prefrontal cortex. The paper was posted to bioRxiv on October 16, not accepted by a journal on that date. Its title, *Rapid alternation of conscious and suppressed stimulus representations in the primate brain*, is an excellent invitation to an argument and a poor license to settle one. [The original preprint](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.15.759299) is evidence to examine, with the qualifications due to a preprint.

The immediate problem is language. A neuron can carry information about a suppressed image without that image being experienced. A decoder may classify which image was delivered, or which image was reported, without locating the subjective event. When a result is described as a conscious representation, the phrase commonly means a representation associated with conscious perceptual reports under the experimental conditions. It does not mean that a recording electrode has found consciousness inside a spike train.

This book begins with that distinction because much of the proposed engineering follows from it. A representation can exist without being globally useful. It can be locally predictive while unavailable for a question, a memory, or an action. It can be active without the system allowing it to revise its current account of the world. Those are experimentally separable properties. They are more tractable than the claim that a machine has an inner life.

## An image can lose without vanishing

Rivalry has a long history because it produces an unusually clean conflict between stimulation and report. Two candidate descriptions of the outside world are supplied simultaneously. The observer's access to them is unstable. Perceptual dominance changes even though the external images may remain constant.

The arrangement is useful precisely because a simple feedforward explanation will not do. The visual input to an eye does not have to disappear when its image is suppressed. The competition occurs within the processing system. But that does not identify a single switch. Suppression can affect different neural populations differently. A population may retain information sufficient for a decoder while losing the integration needed for a person's report. And the act of reporting can recruit motor preparation, working memory, and decision processes that are not the same thing as perception.

There is a methodological trap here. Suppose a scientist asks a participant to press a button whenever the dominant image changes. The presses establish a useful behavioral index. They also introduce a sequence: perceive, decide that the percept has changed, prepare a response, press, and perhaps reconsider. A late brain signal time-locked to the button may be related to the decision or the press rather than to the contents of experience. Asking for a report has become part of the thing being measured.

No-report paradigms try to disentangle the two. Eye movements, pupil changes, or other measures may index perceptual transitions without requiring an explicit report on each trial. But an indirect measure has its own weaknesses. An eye movement does not carry an affidavit of awareness. A decoder trained on report trials can inherit assumptions from the very task it was meant to replace.

A better science refuses to turn an operational label into an ontology. It says: under this masking or rivalry condition, this information predicts report; under another condition it persists despite the loss of report; under a third, perturbation of a particular circuit changes behavior. Then it asks whether one causal model explains the differences more economically than its competitors.

Temporal Access and Continuity Hypothesis, shortened here to TACH, starts from that discipline. Its computational question is whether information can be represented locally, admitted selectively to a broader system, integrated over time, and retained across interruptions in a way that supports flexible behavior. The system need not declare itself conscious. The experimenter need not treat the label conscious as the output of a classifier. We can build and falsify the access machinery without pretending to have a meter for experience.

The distinction between existence and access also appears in ordinary human life, though anecdotes are not substitutes for experiments. A driver may respond to movement on the edge of vision without later remembering an object. A person can read while failing to register a repeated word. Some processing leaves a measurable trace without reaching the level of deliberate description. These examples are easy to overstate; attention, memory, decision, and awareness are entangled in natural behavior. Their value is that they keep the question in view: processing is not a single on-or-off event.

Sometimes the scientific vocabulary makes the separation harder to see. Words such as *representation*, *encoding*, and *decoding* describe relations between signals, experimental conditions, and statistical models. A representation in a laboratory analysis is not a little picture in the head. If a population of neurons permits a classifier to distinguish images, that says something about the information present in the population and the conditions under which the classifier can extract it. It does not tell us how much of that information is available to the organism's own decision processes, which neurons causally use it, or how it figures in subjective experience.

This is especially important with powerful decoders. A classifier trained on thousands of examples may reveal a signal that the animal never uses in the same way. The analysis can be excellent while the interpretation exceeds it. The task of an access theory is to explain the conditions under which information becomes consequential for other processes. It cannot merely point to the fact that a trained outside observer can recover it.

## The wager hidden inside a clock

The interest in rapid alternation comes from a seductive picture. Perhaps a stream of consciousness feels continuous because the brain repeatedly samples, suppresses, promotes, and revisits representations in short intervals. One content set takes the foreground; another waits in the machinery. A later micro-epoch changes the election. Continuity would then arise from the relationship between epochs rather than from the uninterrupted possession of a single scene.

There are reasons to investigate this. Human attention is selective. Working memory is limited. Perception unfolds over measurable time. Recurrent processing repeatedly transforms what a feedforward sweep first made available. In many tasks, access to a feature changes what can be compared, remembered, or acted upon. A temporal system that coordinates those operations is a reasonable engineering hypothesis.

There are also reasons to be suspicious. Every digital computer has a clock, and that has never made clock ticks evidence of consciousness. A network can alternate attention weights for reasons having nothing to do with experience. A system can buffer several representations, process them serially, and produce smooth behavior through ordinary state estimation. A successful model might owe its advantage to a better information budget, not to the existence of phases.

A particularly damaging possibility is that the whole clock story is an artifact of measurement. Imagine two sensors sampling the same changing world at different moments. Their predictions differ because they received different evidence. Calling the better-timed acquisition a special temporal-binding mechanism would confuse data collection with computation. The same error appears when the alternative network is given more parameters, more memory, or privileged access to the answer. It will look as though a theory won when the contest was never fair.

This book will therefore ask what remains after the easy advantages have been removed. If an alternating system receives exactly the same observations, at the same latency, with the same communication budget and available compute as an irregularly gated system, what can it still do that the competitor cannot? If the answer is nothing, the specific rhythm is unnecessary for the task. That conclusion would not settle consciousness. It would settle an engineering claim that consciousness-inspired rhetoric sometimes obscures.

The word *continuity* matters as much as *access*. An experience that lasts long enough to guide action depends on more than a single promotion event. A representation may have to survive a blink, a competing object, a delayed consequence, or a command that fails. An agent may have to preserve a belief about the world while the sensory input changes, then revise the belief when a prediction is contradicted.

In a genuinely difficult task, the issue becomes causal. Did the object move because the agent acted? Because an external disturbance occurred? Because the motor command failed? Because the sensory observation was misleading? An architecture that can answer these questions over time is interesting even if no one calls it conscious. That is the part worth building.

Frequency is the obvious temptation. A result associated with an approximately fifteen-hertz pattern is quickly translated into a sixty-seven-millisecond unit of subjective life. The arithmetic is simple and the inference is not. An oscillation may coordinate communication, mark a sampling regime, reflect neural excitability, or arise from the experimental task. It might be a useful control signal without being a privileged unit of experience. Conversely, a computational architecture could display useful temporal coordination without an identifiable sinusoidal clock. The waveform cannot answer the philosophical question on its own.

The experiments that can answer something narrower are surprisingly demanding. Change phase while conserving the input samples. Change sample timing while conserving the information set. Change recurrence while conserving parameter count. Use a model with no rhythm as the comparator, and allow that competitor enough expressive capacity and training to win. Keep the testing world separate from the world in which thresholds were chosen. Then observe not merely aggregate accuracy but which content is recovered, how soon, at what cost, and whether the recovery changes later action.

A theory that survives those constraints has earned an engineering foothold. It will still owe us the explanation of why a behavioral mechanism should bear on consciousness. That debt cannot be settled by capitalizing the word Conscious in a diagram.

## The evidence keeps the argument honest

The last two decades have produced many impressive observations about neural correlates of consciousness. A correlate is a pattern of activity associated with a condition. A causal contribution requires intervention and a better account of alternatives. Neither is an explanation of why physical processes should feel like something.

This is not a demand for impossible proof before research begins. It is the opposite. When the phenomenal question remains unresolved, operational experiments become more important. A system can pass or fail a working-memory task. Its access gate can be blocked. Its recurrent connections can be delayed. An injected perturbation can be made equal in energy to a sham perturbation. The resulting behavior can be scored. These events can discriminate models even when the philosophical problem remains open.

In April 2025, the Cogitate Consortium published an unusually ambitious adversarial test of global neuronal workspace theory and integrated information theory in *Nature*. The investigators did not deliver a simple victor. Some preregistered predictions were supported, others challenged, and some results proved inconclusive. [Their paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1) is a necessary antidote to the instinct that one good experimental result should end a theory war.

The work also illustrates the value of asking proponents to commit to predictions before data analysis. If every unexpected activity pattern can be reinterpreted as an implementation of a favorite theory, the theory is learning very little from failure. TACH must not acquire that immunity. It needs predictions which can lose, clear rivals, and a history of results that the next revision does not quietly erase.

The first experiments in our own line began modestly: synthetic signals, gates, small recurrent networks, a simulated agent, and explicit counterfactual tests. That is an appropriate starting point for an idea about architecture. It is not evidence that a machine has an experience. The experiments get interesting when they lose. If a purported access advantage vanishes once sampling is equalized, that eliminates a false mechanism. If a network still predicts correctly after its event-triggered transformation is disabled, the transformation has not earned a place in the essential design.

We will return to the actual results, including the disappointing ones, rather than tell a heroic story in which every version proves the one before it. The unpublished laboratory's TACH-6 experiment, for example, found a small numerical advantage for one learned remapping model on a held-out task, yet did not show that its remapping event caused the improvement. The more compelling sentence is the negative one: the architecture had permission to switch, and the evidence did not show that switching mattered.

That is an unusual basis for a book about machine consciousness. It is also a better basis than a sequence of attractive block diagrams. We do not know whether consciousness is a computational property, what the complete biological conditions might be, or whether any present artificial system has subjective experience. There are philosophical positions and scientific theories on each point. Many are serious. None supplies a shortcut past the ordinary obligations of a controlled experiment.

A hypothesis has to make contact with something that can resist it. TACH has several such contacts. It can claim that temporal selection improves flexible integration under a fixed information budget, then lose to a matched Bayesian estimator. It can claim that an access phase matters independent of sampling, then find that its effect disappears when the samples are held constant. It can claim that reciprocal feedback preserves content, then observe that a one-way recurrent system performs equally well. Every loss would be useful, if recorded faithfully.

## What continuity buys

A mind can be imagined as a collection of specialized processes: vision estimates objects, audition estimates sounds, memory retains recent events, a motor system predicts consequences, and an executive process selects actions. That picture describes functional decomposition, not yet the experience of being a person. Still, it raises a mechanical question. How do the parts maintain a consistent account of a changing world when no single process has every piece of information at once?

One answer is a global workspace. Selected content becomes available to many subsystems, so that a percept can alter a memory, a decision, or a sentence. Another is a recurrent hierarchy in which lower and higher areas repeatedly constrain one another. Another is a predictive model that updates hidden causes from observations. These answers need not be mutually exclusive. A good architecture might contain aspects of all three, but the benefits of each must be demonstrated separately.

Picture an autonomous machine carrying a fragile object from one table to another. The object slips behind a barrier. The machine's hand moves, yet the expected contact signal does not arrive. A camera shows an ambiguous shadow. A second camera shows part of the object's edge. The central task is not to announce whether it is conscious. It is to preserve the identity of the object, remember the planned motion, attribute missing feedback correctly, and decide whether to look, reach, or stop.

A purely local visual process might recognize the edge but have no permission to change the active plan. A broad workspace might receive the edge but overwrite an earlier belief. A perfect memory might preserve the old belief too stubbornly. A self/world model might attribute the failed motion to an external cause without enough evidence. Each produces a different failure that can be tested.

Temporal coordination could help. The visual representation may need time for recurrent binding before it is promoted. The workspace may need a readiness condition distinct from the selector deciding which content enters. The motor predictor may need to compare an expected sensation with a later observation. Those requirements suggest different clocks and different kinds of access. No biological finding implies that a software implementation must copy a thalamus, or that the implementation will thereby acquire experience.

This is the sober reason for CHORD, the name of the systems proposal developed in the later chapters. A chord is several notes occupying a relation, not a single note sounding louder. In the proposed architecture, selected content, recent history, predicted consequences, and world-state estimates must coexist coherently enough to guide the next action. The metaphor is useful only if it yields tests. It must be possible to break one relation and predict which behavior fails.

What counts as success? Not a machine saying, with sufficient eloquence, that it sees the object. That would merely add a reporting model to the evaluation. A meaningful success would include preserved object identity across occlusion, an appropriate change of plan when the contact signal fails, a calibrated distinction between a motor failure and an external disturbance, and correct recovery when the object reappears. A second success would show which component was necessary: blocking a route from local evidence to the shared workspace would harm the tasks predicted to require that route while sparing the others.

What counts as failure? It would be a system whose outputs look coherent only because all inputs are available everywhere; a supposedly suppressed branch whose private data leak into the global controller; or a timing result that depends entirely on acquiring different frames. It would be a model that beats an underpowered baseline and loses to a fair recurrent one. It would be a positive-looking accuracy difference whose uncertainty interval spans zero. And it would be an architecture whose named control signal can be removed without changing anything important.

The purpose of building such a system is partly to discover how little of the original theory is needed. That is how an architectural proposal becomes smaller and more useful. If generic recurrence is enough, use it. If a global workspace adds robust transfer, measure the transfer. If a readiness signal helps with resource allocation, do not call it proof of awareness. A good theory should not insist on the ornamental survival of all its parts.

## Where the image returns

The rivalry stimulus has not changed while the observer's report changes. That simple fact has forced scientists to separate input from perception for generations. The new temptation is to say the difference lies in a rapid alternation schedule, perhaps even a particular frequency. That may eventually be supported for specific circuits and tasks. It may also turn out to be one solution among many, or a useful signature of processes occurring for another reason.

The correct position is neither credulous nor dismissive. Hesse and colleagues supplied a reason to investigate temporal selection in neural populations. Shi and colleagues supplied a concrete example of rapid population-code reorganization. Later work supplied a measurable recurrent-binding limit. None supplied the missing bridge from computation to subjective experience.

This distinction is not a disclaimer to be hurried past on the way to bigger claims. It is a productive constraint on the whole project. It tells the experimenter to measure access, interference, readout, causal influence, and continuity. It tells the writer not to borrow authority from a philosophical word. It leaves open a difficult question without allowing the difficulty to license whatever explanation sounds most exciting.

The suppressed image, after all, was still there in the stimulus. The observer could not report it as the current percept. The machine-builder's question is what a system must do so that available information becomes usable across time, and what can be learned when that process is interrupted.

There is an extraordinary amount to find out before calling the resulting system a mind.
