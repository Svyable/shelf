# 13 — Security Theater in White

A bright street feels more managed than a dark one.

The feeling is powerful. A person approaching a transit stop or public-housing entrance after midnight may want to see who is nearby, where the steps are, and whether a route is open. A well-lit place can make hazards legible and reassure people that the public realm has not been abandoned.

The feeling is also an imperfect measure of crime.

Lighting can affect offenders, victims, witnesses, and the use of public spaces. It can change whether people recognize faces or hazards. It can discourage some crimes under some conditions. It can also create glare, deep contrasts, or a false sense of security when the actual environment remains poorly designed.

There is direct experimental evidence that lighting can reduce crime in a particular context.

In 2016, researchers and public partners randomly assigned temporary streetlighting to public-housing developments in New York City. Aaron Chalfin, Benjamin Hansen, Jason Lerner, and Lucie Parker reported the results in a [2019 National Bureau of Economic Research working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25798), later published in the *Journal of Quantitative Criminology*. After accounting for possible spatial spillovers, they estimated that additional streetlighting led to at least a 36 percent reduction in nighttime outdoor index crimes in the studied setting.

That is an important finding. It is stronger evidence for a causal effect than an anecdote about a bright street feeling safe. It is not, however, a universal rule that every additional lamp everywhere will reduce crime by 36 percent.

The treatment, baseline lighting, neighborhoods, crime patterns, and implementation matter. A community with inadequate nighttime visibility may benefit greatly from additional illumination. A brightly saturated commercial strip may gain little from another floodlight.

To design responsibly, we need to distinguish lighting that improves public safety from lighting installed mainly to signal that someone is doing something.

## A randomized street

Urban safety research often relies on correlations. Places with more crime may receive more lighting, making illuminated areas appear dangerous even if the lamps help. Wealthier neighborhoods may have better lighting and less crime for many other reasons. Police activity, traffic, land use, and local institutions can influence both lighting and observed outcomes.

Random assignment helps resolve some of these problems. If comparable sites receive different lighting treatments through a process not determined by their crime risk, a difference in outcomes can support a causal interpretation.

The New York experiment was unusual because researchers partnered with public agencies to install temporary lighting in randomly selected developments. The setting involved public-housing areas and a particular intervention designed to create substantial additional light. The result concerned nighttime outdoor crimes, not every category of crime and not every indoor or daytime outcome.

The study's estimate of a sizable reduction has policy importance because lighting is often discussed as a soft amenity rather than a possible safety intervention. In places with insufficient illumination, better lighting can be valuable. Dismissing the effect because light pollution exists would be irresponsible.

But a rigorous reader must also ask what changed beyond the number of photons. A lighting installation may alter use of public space, visibility, potential witnesses, or offenders' expectations of detection. The mechanisms may interact, and the measured reduction does not imply that each additional unit of brightness produces the same marginal benefit.

This matters when applying results elsewhere. A street already meeting its safety and visibility needs may not respond to a large increase in brightness the way an inadequately lit public-housing development did. The experiment supports targeted lighting in certain conditions, not unlimited illumination.

A useful policy would measure the baseline problem, specify the safety objective, choose lighting appropriate to the task, and evaluate the result. It would not treat brightness as a generic cure for the social causes of violence.

## The British counterexample

A different body of evidence asks the reverse question: what happens when cities reduce streetlighting?

In a [2015 study published in the *Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health*](https://doi.org/10.1136/JECH-2015-206012), researchers examined changes in streetlighting across 62 local authorities in England and Wales. The policies included switching some lights off, part-night lighting, dimming, and changing to white light. The study used a controlled interrupted time-series approach with geographically coded police records of road collisions and crime.

The researchers found little evidence that the studied adaptations were associated with increased nighttime road collisions or aggregate crime. There was uncertainty and heterogeneity, especially for some crime estimates. Full switch-off was relatively uncommon, limiting precision for that category. The results therefore do not prove that every lighting reduction is harmless.

The study is nonetheless an important counterweight to the assumption that any dimming must make streets more dangerous. In the studied settings, reduced lighting did not produce the broad harms that a simple more-light-is-always-safer theory would predict.

The New York randomized experiment and the British observational study are not contradictions to be resolved by choosing a favorite. They examined different interventions, starting conditions, places, and designs. A large increase in lighting at selected public-housing developments can reduce certain crimes, while modest reductions or adaptive schedules in other environments may have little detected effect on aggregate crime or collisions.

The evidence suggests that the relationship between light and safety is conditional. That is a less satisfying slogan and a better basis for public policy.

A city should not use the British findings to darken a dangerous crossing without analysis. It should not use the New York findings to justify keeping decorative office lights on all night. Neither application follows from the study.

## What people are afraid of

Safety is not only the measured count of crimes or road injuries. It also includes whether people feel able to use a place. A route that is statistically safe but perceived as threatening may remain inaccessible to residents, especially those who have experienced harassment or violence.

Those perceptions deserve respect, even when they are not perfect predictors of risk. Women, older people, people with disabilities, and others may face different safety concerns in the same public space. A lighting plan that focuses exclusively on astronomical sky brightness can neglect those experiences.

The British lighting-reduction research included attention to public perceptions as well as recorded outcomes. Some people barely noticed changes, while others expressed concerns about fear, neglect, and local governance. The symbolic meaning of light can be powerful even when the measured safety effect is uncertain.

A responsible process should not tell concerned residents that their fear is irrational because a regression did not find a statistically significant increase in crime. It should ask what visual or environmental conditions contribute to the concern and what interventions could address them.

Sometimes the answer is better light. Sometimes it is clearer sightlines, maintenance, reliable transit, active public use, or improvements in the physical route. Lighting cannot replace those measures, but it can support them.

The distinction between brightness and useful visibility is critical. A bright unshielded lamp can create glare that makes a person harder to see beyond it. A carefully placed fixture may improve facial recognition and path legibility without dramatically raising overall illumination.

The goal is not to instruct residents to accept darkness in the name of ecology. It is to provide environments where people can see and move safely without unnecessary spill.

## Light as evidence of management

An illuminated place can signal that someone is maintaining it. The association has a social history. Streetlighting became part of civic investment, urban modernity, and the expectation that public routes would remain accessible after dark.

That symbolism can create a planning trap. If a lamp is visibly bright, an official can point to it as evidence that a safety problem has been addressed. The installation is tangible. The more difficult work of maintaining public space, reducing violence, and improving mobility is less immediately visible.

The distinction is not an argument that lighting is merely symbolic. The New York experiment shows a measurable effect in its setting. It is an argument that a visible intervention should be evaluated for its actual function.

A public-housing courtyard with poor visibility may genuinely benefit from additional light. A city that has already installed sufficient targeted lighting may gain little from flooding the same area more brightly. A decorative office tower may contribute substantial skyglow while offering almost no public-safety service after hours.

The policy question is not whether light is safe or unsafe. It is whether the installation changes the specific risks and visual tasks of the place, and at what cost.

A city should be willing to increase illumination where evidence and need justify it, while reducing spill elsewhere. That is more demanding than a universal dark-sky rule but more credible to the people who use the streets.

## The glare nobody ordered

Glare is especially important because it demonstrates why more emitted light is not equivalent to better visibility. A bright point source can reduce contrast, cause discomfort, or impair a driver's ability to recognize an object outside the lit region.

Visual adaptation and background conditions matter. A person moving between intensely lit and dark areas may need time to adjust. A streetlight aimed at a driver's eyes can make the scene less legible even while increasing measured light output. Uniformity, shielding, source position, and surface reflectance are part of effective design.

These are technical variables with safety implications. They also align with some environmental goals. Shielded light directed onto the intended surface can reduce upward emission and unwanted spill while improving comfort and visibility.

The challenge is to evaluate a system, not a product label. A fixture with high efficiency and a warm spectrum may still be poorly positioned. A low-output light may be inadequate at a dangerous crossing. A good standard should specify required visual performance and discourage unnecessary glare.

Outdoor lighting is therefore a form of applied human-factors engineering. The user is not an abstract average eye. The street contains people with different visual abilities and tasks, moving at different speeds under different weather conditions.

The safety case for a lamp should be specific enough that an engineer can test whether it serves the intended purpose.

## A city that chooses where to be bright

The most coherent public approach combines both strands of evidence. Where communities lack effective nighttime visibility, lighting can be a meaningful safety intervention. Where illumination is excessive or serves little current function, reducing or redirecting it may produce energy and environmental benefits without measurable safety losses, depending on context.

This is not a compromise reached by splitting the difference between two slogans. It is an evidence-based distinction between situations.

A municipal lighting program could prioritize crossings, transit routes, public-housing entrances, emergency access, and other areas with identifiable visual needs. It could use shielded fixtures, controls, and appropriate spectra. It could measure safety and user experience before and after changes. It could identify decorative or redundant sources that contribute heavily to spill without providing comparable public benefit.

Such a program would resist two political temptations. One is to treat dark-sky protection as an aesthetic luxury more important than residents' immediate needs. The other is to use safety as an unlimited exemption from evaluating any light output.

Neither is necessary. The engineering tools exist to provide targeted visibility while reducing unnecessary illumination.

The more difficult obstacle is institutional habit. Procurement, maintenance, liability fears, and public expectations can preserve old lighting patterns long after their original function changes. A thoughtful redesign requires cooperation across transportation, public safety, utilities, planners, residents, and environmental specialists.

That work is less dramatic than installing the brightest possible lamp. It is also more likely to produce a night that serves its users.

## Why the two crime studies do not cancel out
Imagine two cities. In the first, a courtyard used at night has poor visibility, and researchers introduce a substantial new lighting treatment. In the second, many streets already have established lighting, and officials experiment with modest dimming or part-night operation. If crime falls in the first and does not rise detectably in the second, the findings need not disagree.

The interventions occur at different starting points on a relationship that may not be linear. Moving from inadequate visibility to adequate visibility can have a larger effect than moving from bright to brighter. Reducing excess illumination while preserving functional visibility may have little effect on crime. The particular offenses, locations, and public responses also matter.

The New York experiment was randomized, strengthening its causal interpretation for the public-housing developments studied. The British study covered a wider set of local policies but relied on an observational design, with different uncertainties. Neither is a direct replication of the other.

A simplistic public debate asks which paper proves that lighting is good or bad. A more useful research question asks under what conditions a specific lighting change improves or worsens the outcome being measured.

To answer, future studies would benefit from detailed baseline conditions, lighting geometry, pedestrian activity, and crime type rather than only the total number of installed lamps. A treatment can alter the use of public space as well as visibility. Those mechanisms can be investigated, even when they cannot all be isolated perfectly.

The practical implication is to direct attention to places with unmet visual and safety needs while scrutinizing unnecessary brightness elsewhere.

### A photograph that exaggerates safety

A brightly illuminated place can look reassuring in a nighttime photograph. The scene may show clean surfaces, visible doors, and a sense of order. But a photograph cannot reveal crime risk, social relations, or whether an observer can identify a hazard when approaching from a different direction.

A lamp may also produce a sharp contrast between its pool of light and the surrounding darkness. The photograph can expose the bright area beautifully while concealing poorly lit routes nearby. A driver or pedestrian moving through the space experiences an adaptation problem that the fixed image does not show.

To judge functional lighting, investigators should examine viewing positions and realistic movement. Can a person recognize another person at the necessary distance? Is an exit legible? Does glare obscure a crossing? Do abrupt transitions create problems? Are vulnerable users adequately served?

Some of these questions require technical measurements; others require observation and consultation with people who actually use the space. Neither can be replaced by counting lumens.

The same concern applies when officials showcase a dark-sky retrofit with a dramatic before-and-after image. A darker photograph is not sufficient evidence that users remain safe. A responsible evaluation includes the intended human tasks.

The quality of a night should not be judged from one camera exposure.

### Who is asked to accept the risk?

A dark-sky policy can appear easy when proposed by someone who rarely uses the affected street after midnight. A night worker, a person who relies on public transit, or an older resident may have different experience of the same route.

Their concerns are relevant even when a statistical study finds no increase in crime under comparable policies elsewhere. Aggregate data may not represent the specific location, and perceived accessibility can matter independently of recorded incidents.

A credible process should identify who uses the route and what they need to see. Residents should have an opportunity to describe hazards, but public consultation should not end at collecting preferences for bright or dim. Designers can show how improved shielding, targeted vertical illumination, and more uniform light distribution meet concerns without flooding the surroundings.

The objective is not to force people to choose between ecological restraint and personal safety. It is to find the design that meets the safety objective at the least unnecessary environmental cost.

That can mean more light at a crossing and less decorative light on a neighboring wall. It can mean maintaining illumination along a transit route while dimming an unused parking area. The interventions are specific because the needs are specific.

Safety should be a measurable requirement, not a limitless exemption from thoughtful design.

### Lighting and the appearance of care

Public lighting can communicate that a city has invested in a neighborhood. That communication matters because neglected public spaces can feel hostile or inaccessible. But the symbolic effect can also create pressure to oversupply illumination as a substitute for addressing other problems.

If residents report that a street feels unsafe, the response should begin with the actual conditions: sightlines, maintenance, transport access, crime patterns, pedestrian traffic, and lighting. A brighter fixture may help, but so might removing an obstruction or repairing a route. A city that installs a floodlight while leaving other problems untouched may have purchased a visible sign of care rather than the underlying service.

A good experiment could compare several interventions or use a phased design that allows outcomes to be measured. It should account for displacement of crime to nearby locations and for changes in public use. A reduction in recorded incidents in a treated area is meaningful, but its wider interpretation depends on what happened elsewhere.

There is no reason to treat lighting as a magical social policy. It is one physical variable that can influence behavior. Its effect should be studied with the same rigor as other interventions.

The stronger public message is that residents deserve infrastructure whose performance is demonstrated rather than merely displayed.

### A lighting decision with more than one score

A city changing lights in a housing development could choose several outcomes in advance: nighttime outdoor crime, pedestrian visibility, reported comfort, energy use, complaints about light trespass, and equipment reliability. It might also measure skyglow or habitat exposure where relevant.

The project could improve some outcomes and worsen others. That is not evidence that the evaluation failed; it is evidence that public infrastructure has multiple functions. The decision should make the tradeoffs visible.

A weighted single score can conceal important differences. A project that saves considerable electricity but impairs visibility at a dangerous crossing should not be declared successful merely because the total metric improved. Conversely, a large safety gain at a previously hazardous site may justify some additional illumination even if the regional sky does not benefit.

The purpose of measurement is to tell policymakers where the tradeoffs occur. It is not to manufacture a universal answer.

The strongest approach can also revise itself. If complaints or collision data reveal a problem after dimming, restore or redesign the relevant illumination. If a brighter installation produces no measurable benefit while creating glare, adjust it. A lighting policy should learn from outcomes rather than defend its first decision indefinitely.

That is what makes evidence more useful than slogans about light and darkness.

### What the statistics cannot see

Crime data do not capture every aspect of fear, access, or community experience. Police records are affected by reporting, enforcement, classification, and behavior. A person may avoid a dark route even when no crime occurs there, reducing exposure to risk but also reducing freedom to use the space.

A study focused on crime counts could miss that exclusion. A study focused only on perceived safety could miss a real change in harm. Both need context.

The New York and British studies contribute different pieces of evidence. They should motivate more precise research, not settle every local dispute by citation alone. A public agency can respect subjective safety while demanding objective evaluation of actual hazards.

The argument for responsible lighting is strongest when it refuses to trivialize either human concern or environmental exposure. Some places need more useful light. Many places need less wasted light. The distinction is the work of design.

## Security is not measured in lumens alone

There is a final reason to reject brightness as a universal safety proxy: crime is a social behavior, not a direct physical function of illuminance. Light may change the circumstances under which crimes occur, but it does not eliminate poverty, conflict, social exclusion, or other contributing conditions. A bright courtyard cannot substitute for competent public institutions.

The same applies to road injuries. Lighting can improve visibility under relevant conditions, but traffic speed, road geometry, vehicle design, impairment, and pedestrian infrastructure also matter. A lamp is one part of a safety system.

The New York experiment deserves to influence decisions where additional lighting may produce similar benefits. The British reductions study deserves to prevent unnecessary fear that any dimming automatically creates danger. Their differences should encourage local evaluation, not a universal policy.

A serious dark-sky movement should want people to be safe. A serious public-safety program should want light to be well designed. The two goals become opponents only when brightness itself is treated as the objective.

A lit street can be safer. It can also be unnecessarily bright. We should be able to tell the difference.
