# 14 — The Unlit Road

A pedestrian steps off a curb into a patch of light. A driver approaches, adjusting from one brightness level to another. A cyclist moves along the edge of the lane. The road is illuminated, but that fact alone does not tell us whether the three people can see one another in time.

Road lighting is a practical discipline rather than a contest in brightness. It must help users recognize surfaces, objects, hazards, and movement under changing conditions. A poor installation can create shadows or glare even when it delivers high average illuminance. A good installation can provide sufficient visibility with less total emitted light.

The distinction is important because road safety is the strongest legitimate objection to indiscriminate lighting reductions. A lamp that helps prevent a collision is not waste. A policy that darkens a dangerous crossing without ensuring adequate visibility can create real harm.

At the same time, the evidence does not establish that every road must remain illuminated at one maximum level throughout the night. Traffic volume, road geometry, speed, pedestrian activity, weather, and neighboring land uses differ. Design should respond to those differences.

The [Department of Energy's roadway-lighting research](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/ssl/roadway-lighting-research) emphasizes that LED optics can help achieve required light levels with less total generated light, and that dimming and adaptive controls can reduce waste. The opportunity comes from precision, not a magical property of the semiconductor.

There is no honest way to protect the night that ignores the person trying to cross the road. There is also no honest reason to illuminate an empty hillside as though it were an intersection.

## The driver does not see lux

An illuminance reading on pavement measures the light incident on that surface. It does not directly measure a driver's ability to recognize a person wearing dark clothing, judge a bicycle's motion, or see an object near the road edge.

Visibility depends on contrast. The pedestrian must be distinguishable from the background at the driver's viewing position. The distribution of light matters, including vertical illumination on people and objects, uniformity across the road, and the effects of glare.

A fixture aimed to maximize brightness beneath its pole may produce a bright circle followed by a dim gap. From a driver's perspective, the transitions can complicate visual adaptation and object recognition. A lower-output installation with better distribution may provide more useful conditions.

Weather changes the problem. Rain can make road surfaces reflective and create glare. Fog scatters light and reduces contrast. Snow can brighten the environment through reflection. A lighting specification must consider these conditions rather than assume that the nighttime roadway is a fixed laboratory surface.

Pedestrians also differ in visual ability. Older adults and people with impaired vision may be more sensitive to glare or need greater contrast. A design optimized for a young driver with excellent vision may not serve everyone using the space.

This is why a responsible nighttime policy should not equate protecting darkness with minimizing the number of lumens at all costs. It should ask what visual tasks the road actually requires and then supply sufficient, well-distributed light.

The same precision can reduce environmental spill. When light is directed onto relevant surfaces rather than upward or into adjacent properties, safety and dark-sky goals may align.

## The road after the last bus

Road use changes through the night. A major arterial may carry substantial traffic for many hours. A residential street may become quiet after evening. An industrial road may have heavy overnight freight. A pedestrian route to a transit station may remain important even when nearby shops are closed.

One fixed lighting schedule cannot represent those differences.

Adaptive lighting can vary output according to time, use, or other operating conditions. A simple part-night dimming plan may be suitable where traffic and pedestrian activity predictably fall. A more sophisticated system might respond to measured occupancy, though sensors create their own failure modes and maintenance requirements.

The critical requirement is that a lower lighting level still meets the safety needs of the relevant users. A road can be quiet without being empty. Emergency vehicles, cyclists, and pedestrians may still appear. A sensor can miss an approaching person. A sudden change in brightness can be distracting. A design must consider worst credible conditions, not only average use.

The British research on reduced streetlighting provides relevant evidence. The [2015 controlled interrupted time-series analysis](https://doi.org/10.1136/JECH-2015-206012) examined lighting changes and collisions across 62 local authorities. It found little evidence of harmful changes in aggregate nighttime collision rates for the strategies studied. That does not prove that any unlit road is safe. The study was observational, involved different local implementations, and had limitations in some categories.

Its policy value is that it undermines the assumption that modest lighting adaptation necessarily increases road injuries everywhere. It supports careful testing rather than reflexive fear.

A local road with a history of pedestrian crashes may need better lighting, not less. An empty industrial yard whose floodlights reach nearby bedrooms may need targeted controls. Evidence should guide both decisions.

## Why a spotlight can make a road harder to see

A bright point source can produce glare that interferes with visibility. The effect depends on the intensity, angular position, background luminance, and observer. A driver approaching an unshielded fixture may experience discomfort or reduced contrast, particularly in darker surroundings.

More emitted light can therefore make a scene look brighter without making it proportionately safer. The problem becomes obvious when a lamp shines toward the observer rather than onto the needed surface.

Full shielding, proper aiming, and optical control help address this. Modern LED luminaires can direct light precisely, but they can also create concentrated bright sources if designed or installed poorly. The presence of LEDs does not guarantee reduced glare.

Road design can also use contrast intelligently. Crossings may benefit from illumination that makes pedestrians stand out against their background. Signs and markings can improve recognition without requiring the entire surrounding landscape to be illuminated at high intensity.

A sophisticated safety assessment considers how real people approach a location. A crossing that looks bright from directly beneath a fixture may appear different from a vehicle fifty meters away. A narrow path may be well lit at ground level while leaving faces difficult to distinguish. The observer's position is part of the measurement.

Ecological and astronomical objectives can be incorporated once the human task is defined. A light that never needs to shine into the sky should not be allowed to do so merely because the fixture is cheap. A shield can preserve useful roadway lighting while reducing spill into a stream or woodland.

The most effective intervention may be a different optic rather than a different wattage.

## The lawsuit nobody wants

Lighting decisions are influenced by liability. A city may fear that dimming a road will be blamed for an accident, even if evidence suggests that the existing light contributes little to safety. A property owner may install excessive illumination because being visibly bright seems easier to defend than explaining a careful design.

These incentives are understandable. A tragic outcome can be attributed to a decision that changed the status quo. Maintaining the old lighting pattern may feel safer administratively than making a measured improvement.

But a status quo is also a decision. It has costs: energy, maintenance, glare, unwanted light in homes, ecological exposure, and regional skyglow. The fact that those costs are distributed does not make them unreal.

A better process documents the basis for design. Identify the road function and hazard. Use relevant technical standards. Measure visibility under expected conditions. Consult users. Test changes where appropriate and monitor outcomes. Maintain clear records of why the chosen illumination is adequate.

Such work does not eliminate liability. It creates a defensible basis for responsible engineering.

The temptation to overlight is especially strong when no one is accountable for the light leaving the property. A road authority may optimize the pavement while neighboring wetlands or homes absorb the spill. Environmental requirements can bring those external effects into the specification, reducing the incentive to simply use more output.

A mature lighting practice should make restraint professionally defensible, not a liability risk by default.

## A road with competing users

Pedestrians, cyclists, drivers, emergency workers, and nearby residents do not experience one road in the same way. A driver moving quickly needs to recognize hazards at distance. A pedestrian wants to see a surface and feel able to identify approaching people. A cyclist needs visibility without being blinded by opposing headlights or fixtures. Residents may want the road illuminated without a lamp shining through their windows.

Wildlife may use the same corridor after dark. A light-sensitive bat may avoid an illuminated crossing between wooded areas. Insects may gather around lamps. A stream beneath a bridge may receive spill that alters aquatic behavior.

No single metric captures the whole arrangement. Average lux on pavement is useful but incomplete. Traffic counts are relevant but not the entire purpose. Dark-sky brightness matters beyond the road. Site-specific ecological constraints may be important in some places and not others.

A good project defines these needs before choosing fixtures. It can then search for a distribution of light that serves the necessary tasks while limiting the output that creates little benefit.

This is a different form of economy from buying the cheapest luminaire. It may require more careful design and commissioning, but it can reduce electricity, glare, and maintenance over time. The value depends on site conditions, and it should be tested rather than promised universally.

The road is a shared space at night. Responsible lighting is an allocation problem with safety at its center, not a competition between humans and stars.

## When the road really needs more light

A serious book about light pollution should be willing to argue for additional illumination in some places. A crossing with poor pedestrian visibility may need better lighting. A transit stop used late at night may require a clearer approach. A path with steps or obstacles may be unsafe without task-appropriate illumination.

Reducing skyglow is not an adequate reason to leave those hazards unresolved. The appropriate response is better design.

A project might raise illumination locally while reducing broad-area spill elsewhere. It might install a fixture that directs light vertically onto pedestrians at a crossing rather than over the entire roadway. It might improve signs, markings, or geometry alongside lighting. The result can be safer for people while using less total light than an indiscriminate installation.

The critical principle is specificity. What hazard is being addressed? Which users need to see what? How will the improvement be measured? Where will unwanted light travel? What happens when equipment fails?

These questions make the safety case clearer and give environmental concerns a legitimate place in the same design process.

A road does not become responsible merely by being dark. It becomes responsible when the night is managed to support its actual users.

## The crossing from the driver's position
A roadway can meet a nominal illuminance target and still be uncomfortable or difficult to navigate. The number on the engineering sheet describes one aspect of the light field. A driver approaching a crossing experiences moving viewpoints, glare, contrast, surface reflections, and other vehicles' headlights.

Suppose a pedestrian wearing dark clothing stands near an unlit background. The driver must distinguish the person in time to slow or stop. Illuminating the pavement beneath the pedestrian may not produce the most useful contrast if the person's body remains poorly visible. A different arrangement that provides appropriate vertical illumination may make recognition easier without raising the whole street's brightness.

This is why the evaluation position matters. Measure or simulate what the approaching driver sees at realistic distances and speeds, not only what a technician records directly under the lamp. Pedestrians and cyclists need their own assessments. An installation can perform differently for someone moving in the opposite direction.

Road safety standards incorporate multiple photometric quantities for a reason. Uniformity, glare control, visibility, and task characteristics cannot be replaced by one average. A lighting design that minimizes skyglow at the expense of all those functions would be incomplete.

Conversely, a design that meets visibility requirements with less direct glare and less upward output may advance safety and environmental goals together.

### The road in the rain

Wet pavement changes the optical problem. A dry roadway may scatter and reflect light differently from a rain-soaked one. Pools of water can produce bright reflections, while fog or spray reduces visibility through scattering.

An installation evaluated only on a dry clear evening may behave poorly in the weather when drivers most need visual assistance. A brighter lamp is not necessarily the answer; more light can intensify glare under some conditions. Fixture placement and optical distribution matter.

The road's surface and surroundings can also change over its lifetime. Resurfacing alters reflectance. Vegetation grows into a beam. New signs or buildings change the background. Maintenance crews replace fixtures with models that have different light distributions.

A durable lighting plan anticipates such changes and includes periodic review. A product that met its specification at installation does not guarantee that the entire road will remain well illuminated indefinitely.

This is another reason to prefer performance requirements over raw wattage. The objective is visible hazards under credible conditions, not merely a quantity of emitted light.

### The emergency that defeats a timer

An adaptive dimming plan may work well on nights when traffic follows expected patterns. But a roadway remains part of an emergency network even during its quietest hours. An ambulance, fire engine, pedestrian, or stranded driver can appear when the scheduled use is low.

A design that reduces output overnight should consider those credible events. It may preserve a minimum functional level, provide reliable adaptive increases, or keep sensitive locations continuously illuminated. The appropriate approach depends on road function and risk.

Sensors introduce failure modes. A camera or motion detector may not recognize every cyclist or pedestrian, particularly under poor weather. A control network can fail. Abrupt transitions can distract users. A safety assessment should test the system under these conditions rather than trusting the ideal control diagram.

There is also a maintenance burden. Operators need to know when a sensor has failed and how the lighting behaves during the fault. A system whose energy savings depend on controls that are routinely bypassed will not deliver its predicted performance.

The lesson is not that adaptive lighting should be avoided. It is that adaptive lighting is an engineered service with obligations that extend beyond energy efficiency.

### A place for darkness beside the road

A road can require illumination while the land beside it does not. The edge may contain a stream, wooded corridor, nesting habitat, or residential property. Directing light toward the useful surface can reduce unwanted exposure without darkening the actual task area.

This matters especially where linear infrastructure divides habitats. A row of lights can create a visual barrier across a corridor used by nocturnal animals, even if the road itself is narrow. The response may depend on species and time of night. A lighting plan may preserve a dark passage by adjusting placement, shielding, or operation.

Such measures need site-specific evaluation. A wildlife corridor should not be protected by making a dangerous crossing unreadable to people. A protected dark strip may be possible in one location and require a different approach in another.

The road authority's responsibility should include knowing where the light goes after it leaves the fixture. Meeting the pavement target is necessary for many tasks, but not always sufficient to avoid unnecessary spill.

This is a practical expression of sharing the environment: human visibility is maintained, and the output outside that purpose is treated as something to manage.

### When a lower level works

The British lighting-adaptation study reported little evidence of increased aggregate nighttime collisions across its studied policy changes. That finding supports the possibility that lower illumination can be compatible with safety in some existing road environments.

It does not say which arbitrary dimming level will be safe at a particular crossing. The study pooled different local approaches and had limitations in how specific interventions could be isolated. Local speed, traffic, road layout, and user population affect the outcome.

A municipal pilot should therefore define its starting conditions and outcomes. Measure visibility and use, select locations thoughtfully, and monitor incidents and complaints after changes. Where a safety problem appears, revise the design. Where reduced illumination maintains function, expand the practice under comparable conditions.

The important point is that nighttime road lighting need not be fixed forever at the level chosen by an earlier technology or traffic pattern. It can be evaluated and improved.

A town does not need a philosophical verdict on darkness to decide whether one route has too much glare or another needs better pedestrian illumination.

### The maintenance crew as a safety instrument

The most advanced lighting specification will fail if the field installation is inconsistent. A fixture can be mounted at the wrong angle, a shield omitted, a sensor disabled, or a replacement chosen with different optics. These are ordinary operational problems, not rare scientific mysteries.

The maintenance crew is therefore part of the safety system. It needs clear guidance on what each fixture is intended to illuminate and which spill should be avoided. A road should have records of its critical visibility requirements and the approved replacement characteristics.

A complaint about glare or darkness should trigger an inspection, not an automatic instruction to turn every lamp up. The problem may be direction, obstruction, or local failure rather than insufficient total output.

Similarly, an ecological complaint may be addressed by aiming or shielding a source rather than shutting it off. The response should be proportional to the task.

The best infrastructure is not merely well designed once. It remains understandable and serviceable after the original engineers have left.

### A road with no unnecessary beam

A functional road lighting system has to be evaluated by the people and activities it serves. It should provide visibility where it matters, avoid unnecessary glare, and remain reliable under the conditions users are likely to encounter.

The environmental question adds a second requirement: do not project light into places where it performs no necessary human task and creates avoidable exposure. Those objectives will not always align perfectly, but they can often be addressed through better optics and controls.

This is a more demanding goal than simply lowering total wattage. It asks what each beam contributes and what happens if it is redirected or reduced.

An unlit road is not the ideal. A well-designed road whose lamps know what they are there for is.

## The unlit road is not the ideal

The phrase unlit road evokes both fear and freedom. For some people it means a dangerous journey. For others it means a visible sky and a quiet landscape. Neither impression is a sufficient design specification.

A nighttime road must be judged by its function, its hazards, and its surroundings. Some roads need continuous lighting. Others can operate with lower levels or targeted illumination. Some require ecological precautions because of sensitive habitat. All benefit from avoiding unnecessary glare and spill.

The evidence favors thoughtful design over maximal brightness or universal darkness. The British study shows that certain lighting reductions did not reveal broad aggregate harms in its settings. The DOE research shows that optical control can deliver required roadway light with less generated output. Neither means safety can be assumed without local assessment.

Our goal is not an unlit road. It is a road where every lamp can explain why it is there.
