# Back Matter

## The evidence the night leaves behind

This book has argued for a distinction that lighting policy frequently obscures: *useful illumination* is a service, and *unwanted environmental exposure* is a consequence. Both should be measured. The case is not that darkness is always good or light always harmful. A credible design identifies the visual task, the users, the spectrum, direction, intensity, timing, and the life around the installation.

Research cited in the chapters falls into distinct classes. Randomized interventions, such as the New York public-housing lighting experiment, can help identify causal effects within their settings. Observational policy analyses, such as the British lighting-reduction study, provide wider evidence under different limitations. Field ecological experiments demonstrate local responses, but they cannot supply universal percentages for every species or installation. Satellite and citizen-science observations describe different components of a changing night. Institutional guidance provides operational recommendations, not an exemption from local evaluation.

**No figure in this book should be treated as a universal conversion factor.** The 9.6 percent annual inferred sky-brightness increase is a trend estimated from volunteer observations in the 2011–2022 dataset, not a prediction for every location. The 62 percent fewer nocturnal flower visits and 13 percent reduced fruit set occurred within a defined Swiss experiment. The IARC Group 2A classification applies to *night shift work* under its evidence framework, not any light seen after dark. The 36 percent estimated reduction in certain nighttime outdoor crimes describes one randomized housing-development intervention, not a rule for every additional lamp.

## Questions for the next edition

A future revision should seek independent expert review of the ecological and public-health sections, inspect the full experimental design behind the strongest figures, and update any findings whose status changed after November 16, 2026. More precise local lighting inventories, longitudinal field interventions, and linked measurements of human visibility and environmental exposure would strengthen the practical case.

The book does not claim that one spectral setting works for every species, that every night road can safely be dimmed, or that a darker night automatically restores lost populations. It asks why unnecessary light should be left unexamined when so much of its reach is technically adjustable.
